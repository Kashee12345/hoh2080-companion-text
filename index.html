The Other Doors

The reading for HOH 2080 Holistic Health, Wellness and Medicine

Seven chapters, one for each week of the session. This is the required reading for the course and it costs you nothing. Read the chapter early in the week and everything else, the lecture, the discussion, the assignment, will land better.

Every chapter states a question, works toward an answer, and tells you where the evidence runs out. Where the record is genuinely contested, this book says so instead of picking the comfortable version. Nothing you do in this reader is tracked or recorded.

Chapter 1

What Counts as Medicine

Before you can compare these traditions to anything, can you say what the words mean, well enough that two people arguing would agree they are arguing about the same thing?

I want to open this book with a confession about the subject, and then one about myself.

The confession about the subject is that almost every argument you will hear about holistic health is a bad argument, and most of them are bad in the same specific way. Someone says acupuncture works. Someone else says acupuncture is nonsense. They go back and forth for a while, both getting louder, and at no point does either of them say what they mean by works. One of them means patients feel better. The other means the needles do something to meridians that can be demonstrated. Those are different claims. They can both be true at once, and in fact I am going to show you later in this book that something very close to that is exactly what the best evidence says. But the two people shouting at each other will never find that out, because neither one slowed down long enough to define a word.

So this chapter is about definitions. I know that is not the thrilling opening you wanted. You signed up for a course about yoga and ayurveda and forest bathing, and the first thing I am doing is handing you a vocabulary list. Stay with me anyway, because the definitions are not throat clearing. They are the whole trick. By the end of this course you are going to be able to walk into one of those arguments, ask two or three quiet questions, and dissolve it. That is a genuinely useful thing to be able to do, in a clinic or at a dinner table, and it starts here.

Now the confession about myself, since you are going to be reading me for seven weeks and you deserve to know who is talking.

I am a licensed Doctor of Chiropractic. I adjust patients most days of the week. I also hold a master's degree in pharmacology, which means I spent several years learning in fine detail how molecules change what bodies do, which is not the usual training of someone drawn to holistic health. That combination makes me a strange narrator for this book, and I think it makes me a useful one. I know what it feels like to be inside one of these traditions, to have a patient thank you for something a journal article says should not have helped. I also know how a drug trial is designed and why the design matters.

Here is the part you should write down. Chapter 3 of this book is about the manual therapies, which means chapter 3 is about my own field. When you get there, I want you reading harder than usual. Not because I plan to deceive you, but because nobody is a neutral judge of the thing they do for a living, and the honest move is to say so in advance rather than hope you do not notice. If you ever catch me being gentler with chiropractic than I am with ayurveda, you should hold that against the book. I have tried not to be. I would rather you check.

There is one more piece of honesty to get out of the way. Chapter 3 is my field. The chapters after it are not. I have read the literature on yoga and meditation, on ayurveda, on Traditional Chinese medicine and on the nature based approaches, and I have sat in rooms with people who practice them well. I have never treated a patient with any of them. That difference I am not going to blur. When I write about an acupuncture trial, I am reading the same paper you could read. When I write about what it is like when a patient's pain changes under your hands, I am telling you something I know from the inside. Those two deserve different amounts of your trust, and you can only weigh them if I tell you which you are getting.

A word about the shape of the seven weeks. This chapter is vocabulary and method. Chapter 2 is history: how one tradition out of many became the one we simply call medicine. Chapter 3 is the manual therapies. Chapter 4 is yoga and meditation, which is also where we do the serious work on evidence. Chapter 5 is ayurveda and Traditional Chinese medicine, which make the largest claims in the book and are the hardest to test. Chapter 6 is the nature based approaches. Chapter 7 asks what you do with all of this when a patient wants an answer today. The book gets harder as it goes, deliberately: the later traditions are not stranger, they are harder to study.

The words, and why they fight

Start with the ones you have heard. Complementary. Alternative. Integrative. Holistic. Natural. Traditional. Eastern. Functional. Wellness. Most people use these as if they were a single word with several spellings, a vague cloud meaning not regular doctor stuff. They are not the same, and the differences are not academic.

The federal body that funds and reviews research in this area is the National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health, part of the National Institutes of Health. It is worth knowing that this agency exists, because people on both sides of these arguments talk as though no serious institution studies this material, and that has not been true for decades. The agency publishes definitions, and we are going to use them. Not because a federal definition is sacred, but because an argument needs a shared starting point and this is the most widely used one available.

Their definitions turn on one question, and it is not the question most people expect. The question is not what the treatment is. It is how the treatment is being used.

Complementary means a non mainstream approach used together with conventional medicine. Alternative means a non mainstream approach used in place of conventional medicine. Integrative means a coordinated approach that brings conventional and complementary care together, often with several interventions at once, aiming to treat the whole person and to coordinate care among the providers involved.

Read those three again and notice what is doing the work. The same treatment, the same needle in the same point by the same practitioner, is complementary in one patient and alternative in another. Nothing about the therapy changed. What changed is whether the patient is also seeing an oncologist.

This is the first genuinely useful thing in this book. When somebody asks you whether alternative medicine is dangerous, the honest answer begins with a question back: dangerous instead of what? A woman using meditation alongside her antidepressant and a woman using meditation instead of her antidepressant are not doing the same thing, even though they are doing the same thing. The meditation is identical. The risk is not remotely identical. And almost every frightening story you have heard about holistic health turns out, when you look closely, to be a story about the word instead.

I will say this plainly because it matters more than anything else in this chapter. The documented harms in this field cluster overwhelmingly in the alternative column, not the complementary one. Not because the treatments there are more toxic, though a few are, but because the real injury is usually the conventional care that was declined. That is a pattern worth carrying with you for the whole course, and we will come back to it with specifics.

The words nobody defines

Notice that the agency defined three words off that list and left six alone, and the six it left alone are the ones you will hear far more often. Each is doing a job, usually not the one the speaker thinks.

Natural carries almost no information. It describes where a substance came from, not what it does, how strong it is, or whether it is safe. Lead is natural. Belladonna is a plant. Snake venom is made by a living animal with no help from a laboratory. Meanwhile insulin, which a great many people depend on daily, is made in vats by modified organisms, and nobody who needs it cares. When somebody tells you a product is natural, they have told you a fact about its origin and implied a fact about its risk, and those two are not connected. The implication is the part doing the selling.

Traditional sounds like a historical claim and is usually a marketing one. You will see a system described as five thousand years old, or three thousand, with the number varying by the brochure. Hold that loosely. Most of the traditions in this book were substantially reorganized in the nineteenth or twentieth century, sometimes by one identifiable person, sometimes by a government turning a sprawling folk practice into a teachable curriculum. That does not make them fake. Chiropractic is barely older than the airplane and I practice it. But antiquity is a claim about history, and claims about history can be checked, so we will raise it as a question rather than let it work on us for free.

Here is the rule to take from this, and it disposes of the rest of the list. When somebody uses one of these words, ask what it rules out. Complementary rules out replacing conventional care. Alternative rules out keeping it. Those words have content because they exclude a case. Natural, traditional and Eastern exclude almost nothing, and functional and wellness have no agreed definition at all, so they can be printed on a clinic sign, a supplement bottle or a spa menu without anybody checking. A word that excludes nothing carries no information, however good it makes a sentence sound. Most people using them are sincere, so this is a complaint about precision, not about dishonesty, and precision is all that stands between you and the shouting match I described on the first page.

Holistic is a different kind of word

Holistic does not belong on that list, and the course title puts it front and center, so let us separate it out.

Complementary, alternative and integrative describe a relationship to conventional care. Holistic describes a stance toward the patient. The claim is that a person is not a collection of parts to be treated separately, that the body, the mind, the history and the circumstances come as a package, and that treating one while ignoring the others is bad practice.

Here is what students usually miss. That claim is not in conflict with conventional medicine. It is a claim conventional medicine largely agrees with and frequently fails to act on. Ask any good family physician whether a patient's housing, sleep, work and relationships affect their health and you will get an immediate yes. The complaint is almost never that western medicine denies the whole person. The complaint is that a system built on fifteen minute visits and organ based specialties makes it very hard to treat one.

Make that concrete. A woman comes in with headaches four days most weeks. She works night shifts, she sleeps badly and in the daytime, her jaw aches when she wakes, she is two years into caring for a parent with dementia, and three people have told her that her imaging is normal. Every one of those facts is clinically relevant. Not one is a finding on a scan. A holistic approach, in practice, mostly means the history takes twenty five minutes instead of four and the plan has more than one line in it. That is a claim about time, not about mysticism.

So holistic is not the opposite of conventional. It is a criticism of how conventional care is delivered, and it is a criticism that a great many conventional practitioners share. That reframing will save you from a lot of lazy writing, including some you will find in the popular books on this subject.

It cuts the other way too. Holistic is also what a practitioner reaches for when he wants to avoid being specific. A treatment aimed at the whole person is hard to show has failed, because there is always another part it might have helped. If I tell you I am treating your energy, your resilience and your overall balance, I have given myself a claim that no result can disappoint. A good holistic practitioner can still say what she expects to change and how you would both know if it had not.

Three cases that are genuinely hard

Definitions earn their keep on hard cases, so here are three. I am not going to resolve them for you. Your first assignment asks you to, and I would rather you wrestle with them before you hear my view.

One. A cardiologist finishes an appointment by telling her patient to start walking thirty minutes a day, lose some weight, and take up something for stress, she suggests a meditation app. Is the meditation complementary medicine? She is a conventional physician, it is a conventional recommendation, and the app is the same app a wellness influencer would sell you. Does the label attach to the practice or to the person recommending it?

Two. A man with chronic low back pain has been to a physical therapist, an orthopedist and a pain clinic. Nothing has helped much. He starts seeing a chiropractor and stops going to the pain clinic, not on anyone's advice, just because he feels better and the drive is shorter. He has not refused anything. Nobody told him to stop. Is that complementary or alternative? And notice that the answer may have changed over six months without a single decision being made.

Three. A hospital opens an integrative oncology service offering acupuncture for chemotherapy induced nausea, delivered by a licensed acupuncturist, billed through the hospital, documented in the chart, with the oncologist's knowledge. Is this still complementary medicine, or has it simply become medicine? If the only thing that changed is that an institution adopted it, was the original category ever about the treatment at all?

That third case is the one I would push on hardest. A great deal of what we now call conventional medicine arrived from outside and was absorbed. Willow bark became aspirin. Foxglove became digoxin. The boundary you are being asked to police in this course is not a wall. It is a tide line, and it moves.

Three more, and why hard cases are the test

Here are three more, and they strain the definitions differently. The first three asked where the line falls. These ask whether it was drawn in a world that resembles the one your patients live in.

Four. A woman takes four things she bought without a prescription: a fish oil capsule, a turmeric capsule, a tea for sleep, and an ayurvedic herbal preparation a relative brought back from India. She takes a prescription medication as well, and has never mentioned the other four to a clinician. She would be puzzled if you asked her why, because in her mind she is not taking medicine. She is taking groceries. No practitioner recommended any of it, and no decision was ever made about conventional care, because conventional care never came up. Is this complementary medicine? The definitions assume somebody chose a practitioner and decided what else to use, and here there is neither, yet this is probably the most common form use takes and the form where harms are least visible. One study tested 70 ayurvedic herbal medicine products bought from South Asian grocery stores in the Boston area and found that 14 of them, one in five, contained lead, mercury or arsenic. Almost all of those products were manufactured in South Asia, so the study tells you nothing about products made in the United States. It tells you quite a lot about that woman's shelf.

Five. A hospital tells every employee to complete a mindfulness module at orientation, as part of a staff resilience initiative. The content is what a therapist might teach. Nobody is sick, nobody consented in any meaningful way, and nobody can decline without a conversation with a supervisor. Is this medicine? If it is, who is the patient and where is the consent? If it is not, does that mean nobody has to show it works? Plenty of money moves through practices that are too trivial to require evidence and too mandatory to refuse.

Six. A man is told by his physician to get outside more. He walks in a state forest four mornings a week, and his sleep improves and his mood lifts. His therapist calls this ecotherapy. A friend who teaches yoga calls it forest bathing. His brother calls it a walk. The behavior is identical in all three descriptions. So: is it a treatment? If walking outdoors is a complementary health approach, then so is going to bed earlier, and so is calling your sister, and the category has swallowed ordinary life and stopped meaning anything.

The wrong move, and it is extremely common, is to conclude that because the boundary is fuzzy the distinction is meaningless. There is no clear line between complementary and alternative, therefore the framework is arbitrary, therefore I may believe whatever was convenient to me before the conversation started. That is not skepticism. Twilight does not abolish day and night. A category can have a vague edge and a solid middle, and almost every case you meet in a clinic sits in the middle, where the woman either is or is not still taking her insulin.

Now the part students want to skip. Treat a hard case as a test instrument. When a definition strains, one of three things is happening, and your job is to say which.

Sometimes the definition holds and gives you an answer you did not expect. Case two is like this. The man drifting away from the pain clinic is sliding from complementary toward alternative without anyone deciding anything, and the definition catches that precisely. It feels wrong only because we expect categories to track intentions, and this one tracks behavior instead.

Sometimes the definition is silent because it was built for a world with a practitioner in it. Cases four and five are like this. The federal definitions were written about care, and what the woman with the four bottles is doing is not care, it is shopping. The right response is not to discard the definition but to notice what it does not reach, which tells you where the data will be worst.

And sometimes the definition is wrong at the edge and needs repair. Case three, the hospital oncology service, is my candidate. If adoption by an institution is enough to move a treatment out of the category, the category was never about the treatment, and we should say so out loud rather than quietly re sorting therapies as hospitals change their minds.

So here is the question to ask of any hard case: what would I need to know about this patient in order to classify her, and is that thing worth knowing anyway? In all six cases the answer to the second half is yes. You would need to know whether the man is still being followed for his back, and whether the woman's four bottles interact with her prescription. A careful clinician wants that in the chart whatever we end up calling it. A definition that forces you to ask a useful question has done its job even when it fails to give you an answer.

How many people are we actually talking about

Students often assume this subject is a curiosity, a few people at the margins doing unusual things. The numbers say otherwise, and they say it clearly enough that I want you to memorize two of them.

In 2002, 19.2 percent of United States adults reported using at least one of seven complementary health approaches. By 2022 that figure was 36.7 percent. Use nearly doubled in twenty years, and the increase was not a statistical wobble.

That last phrase deserves unpacking, because the idea behind it will turn up every week from here on. The survey reports the 2002 figure as 19.2 percent with a 95 percent confidence interval of 18.7 to 19.7, and the 2022 figure as 36.7 percent with an interval of 36.0 to 37.5. A confidence interval is the survey's way of admitting that it did not ask every adult in the country. It asked a sample, and a different sample would have produced a slightly different number. The interval is the range the true figure is very likely to fall in, so a narrow one means a large, consistent sample and a wide one is the study telling you to be careful. Put these two side by side. They do not touch, and they are not close to touching, so no amount of sampling luck accounts for a gap that size. That is what a paper means when it reports P less than .001, as this one does: the chance of seeing a difference this large if nothing had really changed is very small. Look for the interval, and notice when two of them overlap, because overlapping intervals are where confident headlines quietly go to die.

Yoga alone went from 5.0 percent of adults in 2002 to 9.0 percent in 2012 to 15.8 percent in 2022, roughly tripling. Meditation reached 17.3 percent in 2022, the most used of the seven approaches that year. These are not fringe numbers. More than one adult in three. If you work in any health field, in any setting, a large fraction of the people in front of you are doing something in this book, and the only question is whether they will tell you.

They often will not, and that is worth sitting with. Patients conceal this from clinicians routinely, usually because they expect to be mocked. A clinician who signals contempt for these traditions does not stop the patient using them. He stops the patient mentioning them, which is strictly worse, because some of these things interact with drugs and a few of them are genuinely dangerous in combination. If this course makes you a more informed skeptic, good. If it makes you a sneering one, I have made your future patients less safe, and I would rather not.

Why patients do not tell you

That paragraph is the most practical in the chapter, so let us take it apart from both ends. Start with the patient, who is almost always behaving reasonably.

A woman has been taking a herbal preparation for sleep for a year. Two years ago she mentioned something similar to a physician, who laughed, not cruelly, just a short amused exhale and a change of subject. She is not stupid. She ran a small experiment, got a result, and updated: the cost of mentioning it was embarrassment and the benefit was nothing, so she stopped. She will not mention it to you either, and you will have no idea that your predecessor spent her goodwill.

A man seeing an acupuncturist for knee pain does not mention it to the surgeon he is waiting to see, and if asked why he would say it seemed rude. He has decided, without putting it into words, that telling the surgeon would sound like doubting him. That is the same instinct that stops people telling their mechanic they got a second opinion.

A third patient conceals nothing at all, because nobody asked. The intake form said medications. She takes no medications. She drinks a tea and takes three capsules she thinks of as food, and the form did not ask about food. If you later discover the capsules and treat her as having withheld something, you are blaming her for your own paperwork. A fourth is simply frightened of the consequences, because when a patient believes honesty carries a penalty, honesty becomes a luxury.

Now the other side of the exchange, and here I speak from my own chair. Patients tell me things they do not tell their physicians, and it is not because I am wiser. It is because I have a longer appointment, I see them over weeks, I am touching them, which changes a conversation, and I have no power over their prescriptions. That is structure, not virtue. A clinician with eleven minutes, a full waiting room and a prescription pad is in a worse position to hear this material than I am.

I will also admit the less flattering half. I have watched clinicians, and been the clinician, who got a dismissive tone wrong. Someone says she is taking something a relative sent from overseas, and there is a flicker of impatience, because what is honestly being felt is not contempt but helplessness. There are forty seconds left, the product has no label in a language anybody present reads, and there is no way to look it up. Contempt is often a disguise for not knowing, and you cannot talk yourself out of an attitude whose real source you have misdiagnosed.

So what should a clinician do differently? Four things, none requiring you to approve of anything.

Ask in a form that presumes use and nothing else. Not do you use any alternative medicine, which asks the patient to file herself under a label she may resent. Try: what else are you doing for this, including anything you take, drink, rub on, or see somebody for? The woman with the tea answers that honestly, because it asks about her life rather than about a category.

Ask early, at intake, when it is routine and nobody is in trouble. A question about supplements that first appears after an unexpected laboratory result reads as an accusation, and will be answered like one.

React to the information rather than to the tradition. Thank her, write it in the chart, and keep your face still. What is in front of you concerns a substance going into a body you are responsible for.

Then separate the two judgments you owe her, because students run them together and get stuck. The first is whether the thing might hurt her, especially alongside what you are prescribing. The second is whether it is likely to help her. Only the first needs an answer today. You can tell a patient honestly that you do not know whether her preparation does anything useful, that you want to find out what is in it, and that your concern right now is one specific interaction. That is not a fudge but the state of your knowledge, stated in order of urgency.

The measure of whether you handled it well is not whether she stops, since she probably will not and in most cases has no reason to. The measure is whether she tells you next time.

Pain is the hinge

One more number, because it explains more about this field than any other single figure.

Among adults who used one of these approaches in 2022, 49.2 percent were using it for pain management, up from 42.3 percent in 2002. For chiropractic specifically, that figure was 85.7 percent. Nearly nine in ten people walking into a chiropractic office are there because something hurts.

Put that beside what you already know about the last twenty years of American medicine and the picture assembles itself. This is the period of the opioid crisis, of rising skepticism about long term pain medication, of clinical guidelines actively recommending non drug options first for back pain. People did not drift toward these traditions because of a vague cultural turn toward wellness. A great many of them came because they hurt, the conventional options had become frightening or unavailable, and they were looking for a door that was still open.

That framing will keep you honest in both directions. It means these traditions are filling a real need, which is a point in their favor. It also means demand is being driven by desperation, which is exactly the condition under which people accept weak evidence and pay for things that do not work. Both are true. Hold both.

The opioid years, and a caution about that story

Let me sketch the context, then say how much weight it can bear. The twenty years the survey covers are the same twenty years in which pain and its treatment became the central controversy of American medicine. Prescribing of opioids for chronic pain that was not cancer pain expanded substantially, on an argument with two parts: that pain was being undertreated, which was true and had been true for a long time, and that the risk of addiction in patients treated for genuine pain was modest, which was not. The second part collapsed. What followed is one of the worst public health disasters in the country's history, and I am not going to put a number on it here, because the numbers deserve a proper source and this is not the chapter where I can give you one. You will have seen them elsewhere. They are not small.

The correction came, and like most corrections it was uneven. Prescribing tightened. Clinical guidance moved toward trying approaches that are not drugs first, particularly for back pain, the most common reason anybody walks into the offices in this book. A lot of people found themselves in a changed landscape without having moved.

Three of them have sat in my treatment room. The first was never offered an opioid at all. She was told to try something else first, which is defensible guidance, and she arrived at my office because a guideline sent her. The second was on long term medication and is being tapered, sometimes well and with a plan, sometimes in a hurry, and he is frightened in a way that makes him an easy customer for anybody promising relief. The third never wanted opioids and had been looking for anything else for years. Those three are not interchangeable, and that matters for how you read the 49.2 percent. Pain as a reason for use is not one phenomenon but at least three.

Now the caution, and I want it as clear as the story. I have given you no figures in this section, and not because I could not find any. The connection I just drew is an inference, not a measurement. The usage numbers are measured. The opioid crisis is documented in enormous detail. The link between them, the claim that people took up these approaches because of what happened with opioids, is a reasonable reading of the timing and nothing stronger. Nobody surveyed those adults and asked whether a prescribing guideline is why they started yoga. Present it as context, and do not attach to it a number nobody collected.

One more thing follows, and it is about ethics rather than evidence. If most people entering these traditions are in pain, and many have been failed once already by a system that promised more than it delivered, the cost of overclaiming here is higher than almost anywhere else. Keep that in view in chapter 3, where it will be my own profession doing the overclaiming.

What evidence means in this field

You are about to be handed the piece of machinery this course runs on, and the last of its three parts is a question about evidence. First you need to know what the words in it mean. There is no mathematics in what follows, only five ideas, each of which fits in a paragraph.

A randomized trial. You take a group of people who have the condition you care about. You split them by chance, a coin flip or a computer, give one group the treatment and the other something else, and at the end you measure everybody the same way. The word that matters is chance. The groups are not sorted by who volunteered, by who seemed likeliest to benefit, or by which patients the researcher liked. Why does that matter so much? Because of confounding. A confounder is a third thing that travels along with your treatment and could explain the result by itself. People who walk in forests do sleep better, on average. They also tend to have time, money, a safe place to walk and legs that work well enough to get there. Watch those people and you cannot tell the forest from the life. Randomization is the only tool that handles the confounders you never thought of, and those are what ruin studies.

The control group. The other group has to get something, and what it gets decides what question your trial answers. Nothing at all. A place on a waiting list. Usual care. A fake version of the treatment. Or a respectable alternative that already works. Those are five different studies wearing the same clothes, and chapter 4 is about the difference.

Blinding. Blinding means the person in the study does not know which group she is in. If the people measuring the outcome also do not know, the trial is double blind. This is not a technicality. What a patient expects changes the pain she reports, and what an assessor believes changes what he writes down, both reliably and without dishonesty on anybody's part. With pills it is straightforward: make a capsule that looks and tastes identical and contains nothing active, which is a placebo, and nobody in the room knows who got which.

Now try that with yoga. You cannot put somebody through an hour of unfamiliar postures without her noticing that she is doing yoga. The Cochrane review of yoga for chronic non specific low back pain says this directly: no trial compared yoga with a sham version of yoga, and participants knew which group they were in. The reviewers downgraded their confidence partly for that reason, alongside some very small trials and inconsistent results. That is no criticism of yoga researchers, who did not choose the universe they work in.

Acupuncture is the interesting middle case, and it is why acupuncture has the most informative evidence base of anything in this book. You can get partway to blinding. Needles can be placed at locations the tradition does not consider active points, and there are retractable needles that give the sensation of insertion without piercing the skin. Whether a sham needle is truly inert is argued about by serious people. But it exists, and so we can ask a question we cannot ask about yoga: how much of the benefit depends on where the needle goes? A large pooled analysis of 39 acupuncture trials covering 20,827 patients found acupuncture better than both sham acupuncture and no acupuncture at all, for every pain condition studied. The advantage was about 0.5 standard deviations against no acupuncture and about 0.2 standard deviations against sham.

Sit with that pair of numbers, because it is the most useful single fact in this course and we spend real time on it in chapter 5. Most of what the patient feels is real. Most of it is not specific to where the needle went. Both halves come from the same analysis, and almost nobody arguing about acupuncture in public has noticed they are quoting one half at someone quoting the other.

It helps to picture the traditions in this book on a scale of how blindable they are. A pill is easy. A needle is hard but possible. A manual therapy, where I am putting my hands on you, is harder. A yoga class is close to impossible. A whole system consultation, where one practitioner prescribes diet, herbs, routine and timing at once, cannot be blinded at all. Here is the crucial point, and students get it backwards in both directions every term. Difficulty of blinding is not evidence that a tradition works and not evidence that it does not. It is a measurement problem, and it tells you how long the question will stay open.

Reviews, pooling, and the size of an effect

A systematic review. Any single study can be wrong, by bad luck, bad design or bad faith, so we would rather look at all of them. A systematic review is not an essay in which somebody who already has a view cites the papers that support it. It is a procedure. The reviewers state in advance what question they are asking, where they will search and which studies count, then search exhaustively and report everything they found, including the studies that contradict their conclusion. Saying all that in advance stops the author going hunting. A Cochrane review, like the yoga one I just quoted, also grades its own certainty as high, moderate, low or very low and says why it was lowered: unblinded participants, no sham comparison, small trials, inconsistent results. A review that shows you its reasons for doubting itself is doing something an advocate never does.

A meta analysis. When the studies are similar enough to be worth combining, you can pool their results into one estimate. More participants means a tighter confidence interval, so pooling buys precision. It does not buy quality. Pool twelve badly designed studies and you get a very precise answer to a badly asked question. Ask what went into the pool. There is a stronger version worth knowing by name: an individual patient data meta analysis, where the researchers go back to the original trials and obtain the record for every single patient rather than working from published summaries. That is what was done for those 39 acupuncture trials and 20,827 patients, and it is why that analysis carries the weight it does. It is slow, it depends on other researchers handing over their data, and it is much harder to fudge.

Effect size. Last idea, and the one students find most alien. Suppose a trial reports that a treatment improved anxiety and the result was unlikely to be chance. You still do not know the thing you most want to know, which is how much. Effect size is the answer to how much, put in a form that lets you compare studies that measured different things on different scales. The trick is to express the difference between the groups in units of how much people varied in the first place. One standard deviation is, roughly, the ordinary spread of a measurement across a group of people, so an effect of 0.5 means the groups differ by about half that spread. By loose convention, 0.2 is small, 0.5 moderate, 0.8 large. Treat those labels as rough guides, and remember that a small effect on something that matters enormously can be worth more than a large effect on something nobody cares about.

With that in hand, look at what a large review of meditation programs reported. Across 47 randomized trials and 3,515 participants, restricted deliberately to trials with active control groups, mindfulness meditation programs produced effects of 0.38 for anxiety at eight weeks, falling to 0.22 by three to six months, 0.30 for depression and 0.33 for pain, with the evidence rated moderate. For stress, distress and mental health related quality of life it was weaker, and for positive mood, attention, substance use, eating habits, sleep and weight there was either low quality evidence of no effect or not enough evidence to say anything.

That is a fair summary of one of the better studied questions in this field, and notice what it is not. It is not a headline. It is a list of modest numbers with their durations attached, some of which shrink when you wait six months, and a set of honest blanks. Chapter 4 asks what an effect of 0.3 feels like inside a life. For now the discipline is this: when somebody tells you a study showed an effect, ask how big, and ask against what.

Those five ideas give you four questions you can ask of any study, on any subject at all. Were people put into groups by chance? Did they know which group they were in? What did the other group get? And how big was the difference? If you can ask those four without notes by the end of the term, this course has done its job even if you forget every tradition in it.

The three questions

Everything else in this book runs on a single piece of machinery, and I want to hand it to you now so you can watch me use it.

For every tradition we meet, we ask three questions in this order.

What is the claim? Stated the way a believer would state it, in the tradition's own vocabulary, at its strongest. You do not get to evaluate a position you cannot articulate. If your summary of acupuncture would make an acupuncturist wince, you are not ready to assess it.

What is the proposed mechanism? The story about why it works. Meridians. Subluxation. Doshas. Vagal tone. These stories matter, and they are not the same thing as the effect.

What does the evidence show, compared with what? Those last three words are the reason this course exists, and they are where most people stop too early.

Here is why they matter, in one example you will meet properly in chapter 4. A large review of meditation programs found moderate evidence that mindfulness meditation improves anxiety, depression and pain. The same review found no evidence that meditation programs were better than any active treatment, such as exercise or medication. Both of those sentences are accurate and they come from the same study.

Now watch what happens to that study in the wild. The enthusiast quotes the first sentence. The skeptic quotes the second. The enthusiast is right that meditation helps. The skeptic is right that it has not been shown to beat going for a run. Neither of them is lying. They are reporting two different comparisons, and whichever one you heard first probably set your opinion.

A trial that compares a treatment with nothing answers the question, is this better than nothing? A trial that compares it with an active alternative answers a much harder question, is this better than what we already had? Those are different questions. Almost nobody tells you which one they are answering. From now on, you ask.

If you learn one thing in this course and forget the rest, learn to ask what the comparison group received. It will serve you in a clinic, in a journal, and in the supplement aisle, and it transfers to subjects that have nothing to do with health.

Two things this book will not do

It will not sell you anything. I have an obvious interest in one of these traditions and I have told you what it is. Beyond that I have no stake in whether you end up persuaded by yoga or unpersuaded by ayurveda. If you finish this course having concluded that most of what we studied is weakly supported, you can still get an A, provided you can say why, with evidence, and can state the strongest case against yourself.

It will also not sneer. Some of what we cover will not survive contact with the evidence, and I will say so when it happens. But a tradition that millions of people use, that has persisted for centuries, and that patients report helping them is not thereby stupid, and treating it as stupid is both unkind and a failure of curiosity. Something is happening when a patient improves. Figuring out what is happening is more interesting than deciding in advance that nothing is.

There is a version of this course that would be much easier to write. It would go tradition by tradition, explain each one warmly, note that research is ongoing, and send you off feeling good. There is another easy version that would list each tradition's claims and knock them down. I think both of those are a waste of your tuition. What I want to give you instead is a way of thinking that works on any tradition, including ones invented after this book is written, including the one I practice.

How to read this book

Seven chapters, seven weeks, one a week, and they are long. You will get more out of reading one in two sittings with a pen than in one without. Here is the machinery from the outside, so you always know where you are.

Every chapter after this one has the same bones. First the claim, in the tradition's own words and at its strongest, which means you will sometimes read a page of me explaining something sympathetically and wonder whether I believe it. Hold that thought until the next section. Then how the tradition is actually practiced today, as opposed to how its own literature describes it, because the gap between those two is often the most interesting thing in the chapter. Then the proposed mechanisms, one at a time and graded. Then the evidence, with the comparison groups named every time. Then harms, both documented and rumored, kept carefully apart. Then Where the evidence runs out, and then Before you move on. By chapter 3 you should be able to predict the next heading, and the repetition is deliberate: a fixed structure is what lets you compare six traditions without quietly applying a different standard to each.

The Where the evidence runs out sections are the ones I would not cut under any circumstances, so let me say what they are for. They are not disclaimers and they are not me covering myself. There are three reasons. An honest field has holes in it, and a textbook that hides the holes teaches you, without ever saying so, that confidence and knowledge are the same thing. They are not, and the difference is most of what separates a good clinician from a dangerous one. Second, those sections are the likeliest place in this book for you to find a question worth your own work. Third, they are where this book's own weaknesses live, and I would rather point at them myself than let you find them and wonder what else I failed to mention. Read them also as modeling. Most students have never heard somebody who knows a great deal about a subject say, in a calm voice, that he does not know. It is a skill, you can learn it, and you will need it the first time a patient asks you something you cannot answer.

A word about the numbers. Do not memorize the decimals, learn the shape: more than a third of adults, one in six doing yoga, about half of all use for pain. I give you the decimals because this book has a rule that every number traces to a source somebody actually read.

Finally, what I expect you to be able to do in seven weeks. Six things. State the central claim of any tradition in this book well enough that a practitioner would accept your summary as fair. Separate claim, mechanism and evidence without being prompted. Ask what the comparison group received and say what difference the answer makes. Read a review's certainty rating and say why it was downgraded. Tell a documented harm from a rumored one and say plainly which you have got, because scaring a patient away from something safe and reassuring her about something that is not are both real injuries. And sit with a patient who is doing something you believe probably does not work, and talk with her about it without lying to her and without humiliating her. That last one is the hardest, it is not on any exam, and it is the one I care about most.

Where the evidence runs out

Each chapter has a section like this, where I tell you what I do not know. Here is this chapter's.

The usage numbers are solid, drawn from a large federal survey with published confidence intervals. But they count self reported use of seven named approaches, and the seven were chosen by the researchers. A survey that asked about different practices would produce a different picture of how common this is. When you see a number like 36.7 percent, it is real, and it is also an artifact of what somebody decided to ask about.

I also cannot tell you, and nobody can, how many of those people are using these approaches as complements and how many as alternatives. That distinction is the one I spent this chapter arguing is the most important one in the field, and it is not cleanly captured in the data we have. I am aware of the awkwardness. I would rather hand you an honest gap than a confident number I would have had to invent.

There is a third gap, hidden inside case four. The definitions in this chapter, and the survey built on them, assume a person who consults somebody. A great deal of real use has no practitioner in it: a bottle bought at a grocery store, a video watched at home, a tea a relative recommended. I do not know how large that fraction is, and nobody does, because it is hard to survey a behavior the person doing it does not call health care. Keep that in mind every time I quote a prevalence figure.

Chapter 2

How American Medicine Became Dominant

If these traditions were pushed to the edges, what did the pushing, and how much of it was evidence?

A student asked me a question a few years ago that I have been stealing ever since.

Her mother was being treated for breast cancer at a large regional hospital, and the hospital ran an integrative oncology service. So twice a month, on the fourth floor of a building with a helipad on the roof, a licensed acupuncturist treated her mother's nausea, and it was charted, and it was billed, and the oncologist knew about it and approved. Across the road from the parking deck there was a strip mall, and in the strip mall there was a chiropractic office, and my student had been going there herself for two years for neck pain. Same road. Four hundred feet apart.

Her question was this. Who decided which one of those goes inside the hospital?

I did not have a quick answer, and I have come to think the lack of a quick answer is the point. Nobody decided. No committee met in 1912 and drew the line that put the acupuncturist on the fourth floor and my student's chiropractor in the strip mall, and in fact that particular line has moved twice in my own career and will probably move again before my student retires. But something decided. Over roughly eighty years, a medical marketplace in which no single profession had the upper hand became a medical system in which one profession held something close to a monopoly on the word medicine, and everybody else got an adjective.

This chapter is about how that happened. It matters for the rest of the course, because every tradition we meet in chapters 3 through 6 is defined, in the language we actually use, by its position relative to the winner. Complementary to what? Alternative to what? The what in those phrases is the subject of this chapter.

I am going to argue that four different forces were at work, that students and popular writers routinely collapse them into one, and that learning to pull them apart is a skill you will use for the rest of your life. The four are genuine scientific progress, the reform of medical education, the power of state licensure, and organized pressure by an occupational group acting in its own interest. All four were present. They are not the same thing, they do not have the same moral weight, and the evidence for each is of a different kind.

Before we start, two warnings, one in each direction.

There is a lazy story told by people who admire conventional medicine, and it goes like this. In the nineteenth century there were many competing medical sects, a sect being an organized group of practitioners with their own theory of disease, their own schools and their own journals. Then science arrived, the sects were tested, they failed, and medicine kept what worked. It is a clean story. It is also, on the timing alone, impossible, and I will show you why.

There is an equally lazy story told by people who admire these other traditions, and it goes like this. The sects were winning, or at least holding their own, until a trade association decided to destroy them, captured the licensing boards, closed the rival schools, and installed itself by force. That story is not fabricated out of nothing. Parts of it are documented, including one part that a federal court found as fact, and we will get to that. But as a total explanation it requires you to believe that nothing of medical substance changed between the era of routine bloodletting and the era of insulin, and that is not a serious position either.

The truth is a weighting problem, and weighting problems are harder to teach than villains. Stay with it anyway.

One more piece of housekeeping, because this book has rules and I would rather follow them in front of you than quietly. I am a clinician, not a historian. The fact base for this course is built on studies I can retrieve and read, and the historical literature is not where my own competence lies. So this chapter is the thinnest sourced chapter in the book. You will notice that I give you very few specific numbers in it, far fewer than in chapter 1, where I gave you percentages with confidence intervals. That is deliberate. I am not going to tell you how many medical schools closed in a given decade, because I have not checked such a figure against a source I have read, and in a book whose whole argument is about standards of evidence, inventing a number to sound authoritative would be the one unforgivable thing. What I can do is give you the broad and uncontested shape of the thing, name the one book you should know about, and be honest about the joints where historians disagree.

The marketplace before anybody won

Let me put you in a room.

It is the middle of the nineteenth century, somewhere in the Ohio valley, and a woman I will call Ellen Hoyt has a fever that has lasted four days. Ellen is invented, but her choices are not. Her husband can fetch any of three people.

He can fetch the regular physician from town, a man with a diploma from a respectable medical college, a member of the county medical society, a reader of journals. This man practices what later writers would call heroic medicine, and the word heroic refers to the scale of the intervention rather than to the courage of anybody involved. He will very likely bleed her, opening a vein to drain off what the prevailing theory understood as excess and corrupted blood. He will give her calomel, a mercury compound, in doses meant to produce copious salivation and purging, because visible evacuation was taken as evidence that the medicine was working. He may raise blisters on her skin. All of this is orthodox. All of it is what the best trained man in the county does, because it follows from a coherent theory about fluids, excitement and depletion that he learned from teachers who learned it from theirs.

Or her husband can fetch the Thomsonian neighbor. Samuel Thomson sold a system of botanical remedies along with the right to use it, and he sold it with an explicitly anti elitist promise: that an ordinary family could learn to treat itself and did not need to hire a learned man at all. The Thomsonian will steam her, warm her, and give her lobelia to induce vomiting. Some of what he does is unpleasant and some of it is risky, but he will not open a vein and he will not give her mercury.

Or her husband can fetch the homeopath from the next town. Homeopathy was founded by Samuel Hahnemann, a German physician, and I want you to hear its claim at full strength, because almost nobody states it fairly. Hahnemann held that a substance which produces a set of symptoms in a healthy person will cure a similar set of symptoms in a sick one, like curing like, and that the medicine becomes more potent as it is diluted, through a process of serial dilution and shaking. He arrived at the first of these by systematic self experimentation, recording in enormous detail what various substances did to healthy volunteers. He arrived at the second partly by theory and partly, I think, by the plain observation that his patients did better when he gave them less. His method required a long, careful interview, attention to the whole pattern of the person rather than the name of the disease, and medicines so dilute that they could do essentially nothing.

Now hold all three in your head at once and answer Ellen Hoyt's question for her. Which one do you want in the room?

From where she sits, the regular physician's advantage is not obvious. It is worse than not obvious. The homeopath will take a long history, be kind, and give her a sugar pellet with nothing meaningful in it, after which she will either recover as most people with most fevers do, or she will not, and either way she will not have lost a pint of blood or absorbed a mercury salt. There are stretches of the nineteenth century where the honest reading is that the gentlest practitioner in the marketplace was the one whose theory was furthest from chemistry, precisely because his remedies were inert and the orthodox remedies were not.

That is an uncomfortable sentence and I want you to sit with it rather than hurry past. The link between having the correct theory and doing the patient good is real but it is not tight, and it has never been tight. A practitioner with a wrong theory and a harmless treatment can outperform a practitioner with a better theory and a dangerous one. Any history of this period that tells you the regulars deserved to win because they were right about the body has to explain why being right about the body was, for a long stretch, compatible with hurting people.

So picture the field. Regular physicians, who had the social prestige and the county societies and whose therapeutics were often actively harmful. Homeopaths, with their own colleges, their own hospitals, their own journals and a clientele that skewed educated and urban. Eclectics, who used botanical remedies but organized themselves as a professional school with faculties and degrees, and who admitted women to medical training well before the regular schools would. Hydropaths, who treated with baths, wrapping, rest, plain food and exercise, and whose regimen looks, from here, like a reasonable program of supportive care dressed in a theory about water. Patent medicine vendors selling bottled compounds by mail, some inert, some alcoholic, some containing opiates. Midwives, who attended most births. Bonesetters. Later in the century, osteopathy, founded by Andrew Taylor Still in Missouri on the claim that disturbed structure impedes the body's own capacity to heal and that manipulation restores it. Later still, chiropractic, founded by D. D. Palmer in Davenport, Iowa, on a related claim about interference with the nervous system.

Chiropractic is my own field, and chapter 3 is where I examine it properly and where I have asked you to read me harder than usual. Here it appears only as one case among several, and the only thing I want from it in this chapter is that it is a tradition which was supposed to disappear and did not.

Two features of that marketplace matter most for what follows.

The first is that the sects were not fringe in the way the word sect now suggests. They had degree granting colleges, hospitals, state societies, textbooks and legislative allies. A nineteenth century homeopath was not a man on a street corner. He was often a respectable professional with a shingle, a practice and a better educated clientele than his orthodox competitor across the square.

The second is that for a long stretch of the first half of the nineteenth century, licensing had largely been dismantled. Many states repealed or hollowed out the licensing statutes they had, in a period of broad political hostility to monopoly and credentialed privilege of all kinds. That is the regulatory vacuum in which the sects flourished. If you want one sentence for why American medicine was a crowded marketplace while European medicine was not, it is that Americans at that moment distrusted licensed monopolies more than they distrusted quacks.

Here is a word you need before we go further, and it is the word in this chapter's title. Allopathic. Students use it as a neutral synonym for conventional medicine, and it is not neutral at all. It was coined by Hahnemann, from Greek roots meaning other and suffering, to label physicians who treated a disease with something unlike it, in contrast with his own like cures like. In other words, the standard term for mainstream medicine in all of our course readings is a name invented by its competitor, and the regulars themselves rejected it for exactly that reason. They did not consider themselves practitioners of a system. They considered themselves practitioners of medicine, and the sects practitioners of systems.

That is not a trivia item. It is the whole argument in one word. The ability to be the unmarked category, the thing that needs no adjective, is the prize that was won over the decades either side of the turn of the twentieth century, and it is worth more than any individual treatment.

What Starr means by sovereignty

Which brings us to the book you should know about.

In 1982 the sociologist Paul Starr published The Social Transformation of American Medicine: The Rise of a Sovereign Profession and the Making of a Vast Industry. It won a Pulitzer Prize, it is still the first thing people hand you when you ask how American medicine came to look like this, and the argument in its subtitle is the argument I want you to be able to state and then weigh.

As the argument is commonly summarized, Starr holds that American physicians achieved something more than prosperity and more than market share. They achieved cultural authority: the recognized right to define what counts as illness and what counts as legitimate treatment.

I want to go slowly here, for two reasons. The first is that cultural authority is the kind of phrase that slides past a reader without depositing anything. The second is a rule of this book. I have not read Starr's pages closely enough to quote him, and the fact base for this course flags his central claim as a common summary that still needs checking against his text. So you will find no quotation from Starr in this chapter and no page numbers, and you should treat what follows as Starr's argument, attributed to him, rather than as an established finding. That distinction is going to matter again in a moment.

Now, what is cultural authority, in a clinic, on a Tuesday?

It is the fact that when you are too sick to work, your employer does not want your account of how you feel. Your employer wants a note from a physician. Your pain, your fatigue, your dizziness, these are real to you, but they do not become officially real, real in a way that moves money and excuses obligations, until a particular kind of licensed professional writes them down in a particular vocabulary. It is the fact that an insurer will pay for a treatment when it carries a diagnostic code and refuse the same treatment when it does not. It is the fact that a disability determination, a prescription, a work restriction, a return to play decision after a concussion, a death certificate, and the legal power to say that a person's suffering is or is not a disease, all run through one credential.

That is authority of a different species from being good at your job. A skilled carpenter has expertise. He does not have the power to decide what counts as a house.

Starr's argument, as I understand it, is that this authority was built, not simply earned, and that it was built through specific institutional work: the consolidation of professional organization, the control of medical education, the capture of licensure, the alliance with hospitals, and the defeat or absorption of rival occupations. On that account, the sects were not refuted so much as redefined. If you hold the recognized right to say what legitimate treatment is, you never have to beat a competitor in a trial. You simply declare that what he does is something other than treatment, and the burden of proof lands on him in a court you control.

You can see why this argument is attractive to people who practice the traditions in this course, and you can see why it gets abused. So let me be fair to Starr, who is not writing a conspiracy book, and fair to the physicians, who are not cartoon villains.

First, cultural authority is not a trick. Authority of this kind requires public consent, and the public consented for reasons. Medicine really did become more competent and more useful across this period. A profession that had nothing to offer could not have held this position for a century.

Second, cultural authority does real work that we want done. When a physician tells a frightened family that the lump is benign and they can go home, she is spending exactly this authority, and it is a good thing that she has it. When a public health officer says a vaccine is safe, the fact that this statement lands with more weight than a stranger's blog post is not a bug. Authority is how a society acts on knowledge it cannot each individually verify. The problem with authority is not that it exists. It is that authority concentrated in one occupational group will be used to serve that group's interests as well as the public's, and there is no mechanism that reliably tells those two uses apart from the inside.

Third, and this is the part students most often miss, Starr's argument about authority is a historical and sociological claim, and historians argue about its weight. It is not a verdict about whether any particular treatment works. Nothing in Starr tells you whether acupuncture relieves chronic pain. That question is settled, insofar as it is settled, by the kind of evidence we met in chapter 1 and will meet properly in chapter 5, and you cannot shortcut it with a story about professional power, in either direction.

Write that on something you will see again. A story about how a profession gained power is not evidence that its rivals were right.

Education reform and what it closed

The most consequential thing that happened to the sects was not an argument about theory. It was a change in what it cost to run a medical school.

Through most of the nineteenth century, an American medical school was typically a proprietary school, which means a business owned by its own faculty, funded by student fees, and sharing out the proceeds among the professors who taught the courses. The implications follow directly. Admitting more students meant more income. Failing students meant less. Terms were short, instruction was mostly lecture, laboratory work was minimal to absent, and clinical experience varied from substantial to essentially theoretical. There were good schools and bad ones, and this was true on both sides of the sectarian line. Some regular schools were diploma mills. Some sectarian schools were serious.

Against that background a different model emerged, built on the German universities, and its American exemplar was the medical school at Johns Hopkins. The model required a college education before entry, a curriculum grounded in laboratory sciences, clinical teaching in a hospital under supervision, and faculty on salary who did research rather than professors paid out of ticket sales. It was unmistakably better training. It was also drastically more expensive, because laboratories and teaching hospitals cost money that student fees alone cannot raise.

Early in the twentieth century the American Medical Association, which had been founded in the middle of the previous century and had spent decades as a relatively weak federation, established a council concerned with medical education and began rating schools. Shortly after, the Carnegie Foundation funded a survey of medical education in the United States and Canada, carried out by Abraham Flexner, who was an educator rather than a physician. His report, published in 1910, is the single most cited document in this whole story. It visited schools, described what it found in blunt and often withering terms, named institutions, and argued for a system of fewer, better, university affiliated schools built on the laboratory model.

What followed over the next fifteen years or so is not seriously disputed in outline. Schools closed or merged. Entry requirements rose. State boards tightened. The training of a physician became longer, more scientific, more standardized and far more expensive, and the number of new physicians produced each year fell for a period.

Now, here is the analytical move that this chapter exists to teach, so I am going to be very direct about it.

Raising standards and excluding competitors were, in this episode, the same action.

Not two actions, one noble and one cynical, that we could separate if we had better records. One action with two faces. A laboratory requirement makes a graduate better prepared and simultaneously makes it impossible to run a school out of a rented hall on fee income. A college prerequisite screens for preparation and simultaneously screens for family money. A requirement that students see patients in a teaching hospital improves training and simultaneously means a school must be near, and allied with, a large institution, which is to say in a city, with capital.

You will want to resolve this by asking about motive. Did the reformers intend to crush the sects, or did they intend to produce competent physicians? I am going to tell you that this question is mostly unanswerable and mostly the wrong question. Private motives of people long dead are not recoverable, and in my experience of how human beings actually work, mixed motives are the norm rather than the exception. A man can believe sincerely that calomel dosing is barbarism, that homeopathic dilutions are absurd, that his own profession deserves higher pay and more respect, and that laboratory training is the future, all on the same morning, and he will not himself be able to tell you which belief is doing the work.

So ask about consequences instead. Consequences are recoverable, and we keep both columns.

In the first column, real gains. The reformed schools taught anatomy, physiology, pathology, bacteriology and pharmacology in laboratories where students handled the material. Graduates understood infection, asepsis and dosing in a way their predecessors did not. Standardization meant a patient could form a reasonable expectation of what a diploma meant. If I had to choose between being operated on by a graduate of the reformed system or by a graduate of the one it replaced, I do not have to think about it for even a second. Anyone who tells you this reform was merely a land grab is asking you to ignore the operating table.

In the second column, real losses, and they fell in a pattern.

The women's medical colleges, which had been among the few routes into the profession for women, largely did not survive the consolidation. The argument of the time was that women could now enter the reformed coeducational schools, which was true on paper and in practice produced a profession that stayed overwhelmingly male for two more generations.

The small number of schools training Black physicians were almost all closed in the reform era. Two survived, Howard and Meharry, and for most of the twentieth century they carried a share of the training of Black physicians in this country that is wildly out of proportion to their size. The consequence of the closures was fewer Black physicians, in a segregated system where Black patients often could be treated by nobody else, and the downstream effects of that are not a historical curiosity. They are a present tense problem in American health care.

The night schools, the rural schools, and the schools that admitted working men who could not afford four years of college first, closed too. Which brings me to another invented but entirely representative person. Call him Marcus Webb, twenty four years old, from a county with nine hundred people in it and no physician. In an earlier decade Marcus apprentices with the doctor two towns over, attends a short course at a proprietary school, passes whatever examination his state requires, and goes home to practice, where he is not as good as a Hopkins graduate and is enormously better than the nothing his county currently has. In the reform era, that path is closed, and Marcus is a farmer. The Hopkins graduate, meanwhile, does not move to Marcus's county. He moves to the city where the hospital is.

That tradeoff, fewer and better practitioners against more and worse ones, is not a nineteenth century curiosity either. It is live right now in every argument about scope of practice, about nurse practitioners in rural clinics, about international medical graduates, about who may give an injection or read an image. The shape of the argument has not changed in a hundred years, and if you work in health policy you will have it over and over.

And the sectarian schools? They could not meet laboratory requirements on fee income any more than the weak regular schools could. Most closed. The traditions that survived with institutions intact did so by reorganizing on the reformers' terms, which is a quietly remarkable thing to notice. Osteopathy is the clearest case: it adopted the scientific curriculum, lengthened and standardized its training, built its own accreditation and licensing structures, and over the twentieth century became a parallel profession with full practice rights, to the point where a student today may not be able to tell you the difference between the two medical degrees her physicians hold. Chiropractic took a different route, keeping a narrower scope and a separate identity, with its own colleges, accreditation and state boards.

If you want one lesson from this section, it is that the reform did not primarily defeat the sects by refuting them. It changed the price of admission to the field, and most of them could not pay.

Licensure, and who writes the rules

Educational reform changed who could be trained. Licensure changed who could legally practice, and licensure is where the story acquires teeth, because a licensing statute is not an opinion. It is a law with a prosecutor attached.

Start with what a license actually is. A licensing statute does two separate things that students constantly run together. It says who may practice, by requiring a credential and an examination. And it says what each license permits its holder to do, which is called scope of practice. Both halves are decided by legislatures, usually on the advice of a board, and the board is typically made up of members of the profession being regulated.

Read that last sentence again, because it is the hinge of this whole chapter. Over the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, American states rebuilt the licensing systems they had earlier torn down. Boards were established, examinations required, and graduation from an approved school made a condition of sitting the examination. Courts upheld the authority of states to do this, which was not a foregone conclusion and which settled an important question in the profession's favor. And the lists of approved schools came, in practice, to lean heavily on ratings and standards produced by the profession's own organizations.

So the private judgment of an occupational group acquired the force of law.

I want to be careful and fair about this, because the obvious reaction is outrage and the obvious reaction is too simple. Delegating technical judgment to experts is normal, necessary and usually sensible. I do not want my legislature deciding by vote which pharmacology curriculum is adequate. Neither do you, and neither does any patient who thinks about it for a minute. The people who know whether a school can teach bacteriology are people who know bacteriology, and they are, inescapably, members of the profession.

The problem is structural rather than moral. When the body that certifies competence is composed of the certified competitors, there is no mechanism inside the arrangement that distinguishes a standard raised for the patient's sake from a standard raised for the board members' sake. Both look identical in the minutes. Both are defended in the same language, which is always and only the language of public protection. You cannot tell them apart by reading the stated rationale, because the stated rationale is the same either way, and I would add that board members themselves often cannot tell them apart from the inside. That is not cynicism about physicians. It is a general claim about human beings in positions where their interest and their judgment point the same direction.

Now watch the two edges of this instrument, because licensure cuts both ways and most students only ever hear about one.

The excluding edge first. Once practicing medicine without a license is a criminal offense, and once medicine is defined broadly, every irregular practitioner is one complaint away from a prosecution. Across the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, irregular practitioners of various kinds were prosecuted on exactly that theory, and early chiropractors in particular served jail time rather than stop practicing. Whatever you conclude about the theory they were defending, the willingness to be jailed for it is a historical fact worth knowing, and it tells you something about how the law was being used.

The admitting edge second. The counter move available to an excluded tradition in the American system is not to win a scientific debate. It is to go to a state legislature and obtain a licensing act of its own, with its own board, its own examination and its own defined scope. And that, over the twentieth century, is what happened, one state and one tradition at a time. Osteopathy, chiropractic, naturopathy, acupuncture, massage therapy, midwifery: the route to legitimacy in this country runs through statehouses. The same instrument that was used to criminalize these practitioners is the instrument that eventually licensed them.

This is why American holistic health looks the way it does, and I cannot stress enough how much it explains. Consider another invented patient, Denise Alvarez, fifty one, with chronic neck pain and a job that has just moved her across a state line. In her old state, her chiropractor took her imaging in his own office, her acupuncturist was licensed and partly covered by her plan, and the naturopath her sister sees could prescribe a short list of medications. In her new state, the chiropractic scope is written differently, acupuncture is regulated under a different board with different supervision requirements, and naturopaths have no licensing act at all, so the woman practicing as one has no defined scope and no board to answer to.

Nothing about Denise changed. Nothing about her neck changed. No study was published on the drive. The evidence for every one of those treatments is exactly what it was three hundred miles ago. What changed is which legislature wrote the rules.

Hold that example next to the three questions from chapter 1, and you will see why I keep insisting that legitimacy and evidence are different variables. Legitimacy is conferred. Evidence is found. They are correlated, loosely, because legislatures are not indifferent to evidence and because professions that accumulate evidence tend to accumulate political strength. But they come apart constantly, in both directions, and a student who cannot tell them apart will spend her career confusing what is permitted with what is true.

The traditions that did not disappear

By the middle of the twentieth century, the thing we now call conventional medicine had the hospitals, the research funding, the insurance system, the schools, the licensing boards and the unmarked noun. By every institutional measure the sects had lost.

They did not, however, go away, and the ways they persisted are instructive.

Some persisted as separate licensed professions, which is the chiropractic and osteopathic story, though those two went in opposite directions: osteopathy merged toward the mainstream until the merger was nearly complete, and chiropractic stayed outside with a narrower scope and a stronger separate identity.

Some persisted by absorption, which is the most interesting and least discussed route. The regulars abandoned bleeding and heroic dosing. Hydropathy's sensible core, rest, graded exercise, water, hygiene, attention to regimen, was taken up by physical therapy, rehabilitation and the whole modern apparatus of lifestyle advice. Botanical remedies came back as the supplement industry, regulated under a different law and sold in the same grocery stores. When a tradition's good ideas are adopted and renamed, the tradition looks defeated in the institutional record and looks victorious in the examination room, and both appearances are accurate.

Some persisted as practice without institutions, carried by patients rather than professions, until the law caught up with them decades later.

And there is one more piece of this story that belongs here, because it is the single best documented instance of the fourth force, organized professional pressure. In 1987, in litigation brought by chiropractors, a federal court found that the American Medical Association had engaged in an unlawful conspiracy to boycott and contain the chiropractic profession. The details, the specific conduct found, the remedy ordered, and what the finding did and did not cover, are in the companion volume to this book, where they belong, since that book is about chiropractic. I am citing it here for one reason only.

It establishes that organized professional pressure against a rival tradition is not a paranoid hypothesis. In at least one instance, in a specific period, against a specific profession, it was found as fact by a court applying antitrust law, which is the body of law concerned with illegal restraints on competition.

Now the discipline. That finding is about conduct, not about efficacy. It does not show that spinal manipulation works. It does not show that chiropractic theory is correct. It does not show that any other tradition was suppressed, in any other period, by any other group. And it is not a license to explain away every piece of inconvenient evidence as the product of bias. I have watched practitioners in my own field do exactly that, and it is intellectually fatal, because a profession that can explain away any negative result has made itself unfalsifiable and has stopped being able to learn anything.

One last observation before we turn to the analytical framework, and it is the one that makes this chapter matter for the whole course. The traditions that were pushed to the margins of the American medical system are, in 2026, used by a third of the adult population.

Recall the numbers from chapter 1. In 2002, 19.2 percent of United States adults reported using at least one of seven complementary health approaches. By 2022 that figure was 36.7 percent. Yoga went from 5.0 percent of adults in 2002 to 9.0 percent in 2012 to 15.8 percent in 2022. Meditation reached 17.3 percent in 2022, the most used of the seven that year. Of adults using chiropractic care in 2022, 85.7 percent were using it for pain management, the highest share of any of the seven.

Marginal, in the institutional sense this chapter has been describing, does not mean rare. It means outside the hospital, outside the insurance code, outside the unmarked noun. One third of American adults are doing something that the system that won this fight still describes with an adjective, and the winner's own definitions, the federal ones we learned in chapter 1, now have a category called integrative whose entire purpose is to bring some of it back inside. My student's acupuncturist on the fourth floor is what the tide line looks like while it is moving.

Four forces, told apart

Here is the toolkit. I have been building toward it the whole chapter, and I want you to be able to run it on any tradition, in any country, in any century, including on a practice that gets marginalized ten years from now and including on my own.

When a medical tradition loses ground, four different things may be doing the work. For each one, there is a specific question that tells you whether it was actually present, and specific evidence that would answer it.

Force one: genuine scientific progress. The content of medicine got better, the rival's claims were tested, and they failed, or the mainstream's results improved enough that patients moved on their own.

The diagnostic question is: was the rival's central claim ever put to a test that it could have failed, and did it fail? And separately: did outcomes improve, measurably, for patients of the winning side?

The evidence that answers it is experimental and clinical. Trial results. Mortality data. Whether a mechanism survived investigation.

And here is the timing problem I promised you at the start, which is, I think, the most useful single observation in this chapter. The decisive institutional victory, the educational reform, the licensing acts, the consolidation of authority, happened substantially before the therapeutic revolution. The treatments that make modern medicine obviously, undeniably superior at curing disease, the antitoxins, insulin, the sulfonamides, penicillin and what followed, arrived after the structure of professional dominance was already in place. The profession achieved sovereignty and then became able to cure things.

So the clean story, that science arrived and the sects were refuted, does not work as a complete explanation, because the chronology runs the wrong way. The winner's strongest evidence showed up after the match.

Do not overread that, though, because the enthusiasts do. Force one was not absent before the drug revolution. Anesthesia was real. Antisepsis and asepsis were real and they transformed surgery from a last resort into a reasonable plan. Germ theory was real and it reorganized medicine's understanding of causation in a way that no sect matched. Diagnostic precision improved. Public health work on water, sewage and food did more for mortality in this period than any clinic did. The honest statement is that there were real scientific gains during the decisive period, that they were mostly in understanding, prevention and surgery rather than in cures, and that they were not by themselves sufficient to explain the scale of the institutional victory.

Force two: educational reform. Training was standardized, lengthened and made more expensive, and schools that could not meet the new requirements closed.

The diagnostic question is: did that school close because it could not teach, or because it could not pay for a laboratory?

Those are different answers with different implications, and the records often let you distinguish them. A school shut down after inspectors found no cadavers, no laboratory and no hospital affiliation is one story. A serious small school that closed because it could not raise capital for facilities after the standards changed is a different story. Both happened.

Notice too that force two acts on supply and on the composition of the profession, not on the truth of anybody's theory. A reform that closes every school training women and nearly every school training Black physicians has not thereby tested a single clinical claim. Standards and exclusion rode in the same cart, as I have said, and the only way to think clearly about it is to refuse to collapse them.

Force three: state licensure. The law defined who may practice and what each practitioner may do, and the law was enforceable.

The diagnostic questions are: what exactly did the statute forbid, and who sat on the board that decided which schools and which practices were approved?

This is the force with the best paper trail, which is why I like teaching it. Statutes, board compositions, scope definitions, approved school lists and prosecutions are all public documents. If you want to know whether a tradition was legally excluded, you can look it up, and you do not have to speculate about anybody's intentions.

Remember that force three also runs in reverse, as the admitting edge. A tradition with a licensing act, a board and a defined scope has been granted legitimacy by the state regardless of its evidence base, and a tradition without one lacks legitimacy regardless of its evidence base. Denise crossing a state line is force three, operating in both directions, on a Tuesday, in this century.

Force four: organized professional pressure. An occupational group acting collectively in its own interest to contain or destroy a competitor.

The diagnostic question is the strictest of the four: is there documented action? A code of ethics provision forbidding members from consulting with irregular practitioners. A policy of denying hospital privileges. A committee whose charter is containment. A court finding. A boycott.

And the rule that goes with it, which I will state as plainly as I can: the fact of a tradition's decline is not evidence that it was suppressed. Decline is the thing to be explained. Reaching for suppression because a tradition declined, with no documented conduct, is circular reasoning, and it is the most common error made by people who write sympathetically about these traditions. When you do have documentation, as in the 1987 antitrust finding, say what it covers and stop there.

Now put the four together on a worked case, because a framework you have never run is not yet a skill.

Take homeopathy, which went from having its own colleges, hospitals and a prosperous clientele to near invisibility in American institutional medicine within a few decades. Run the four.

Force one is clearly present. Homeopathy makes a testable claim about serial dilution, and that claim sits very badly indeed with what chemistry established about matter. Unlike most of what we study in this course, the central mechanism here is not merely unproven, it conflicts with well established physical science. A student who concludes that force one did substantial work against homeopathy is standing on solid ground.

Force two is clearly present. Homeopathic colleges faced the same capital problem as everyone else and mostly could not fund laboratory and hospital based training.

Force three is clearly present. Approved school lists and licensing examinations built around the reformed curriculum made a homeopathic diploma a weaker and then a useless credential.

Force four is present too. The regular profession's ethical codes had long restricted consultation with irregular practitioners, and the social and institutional pressure on physicians to disavow sectarian affiliation was real.

So all four. And then the complication that makes this my favorite teaching case: there is a fifth consideration, which is that homeopathy's single largest practical advantage disappeared when the regulars stopped bleeding and purging their patients. Homeopathy's inert remedies looked wonderful next to calomel. They look like nothing next to penicillin. One serious argument is that homeopathy declined less because it was refuted than because its competitor stopped poisoning people, which removed the comparison that had made homeopathy attractive in the first place. If that sounds familiar, it should. It is the lesson of chapter 1, arriving from an unexpected direction. What the comparison group received decides the result, and that is as true of a century long competition between professions as it is of a randomized trial.

I do not know how to assign percentages to those four forces plus the fifth consideration, and I am suspicious of anyone who does. What I can tell you is that a student who can name all four, say what evidence would establish each, and resist the temptation to pick one, is thinking better about this than most of what you will read on the subject.

Two final disciplines, one for each kind of reader in my classroom.

If you came into this course sympathetic to these traditions, the framework constrains you as follows. Demonstrating forces two, three and four does not rescue a claim that fails force one. If a tradition's central mechanism has been tested and does not hold up, the fact that its schools were also closed unfairly and its practitioners also prosecuted unjustly does not make the mechanism true. Injustice and error are independent. A tradition can be both wronged and wrong.

If you came into this course skeptical, the framework constrains you as follows. Force one was not doing most of the work during the decisive period, and the current institutional position of a practice is therefore weak evidence about its merit. Something being outside the hospital tells you about the history of American licensure and professional organization. It does not tell you whether it helps a patient with chronic low back pain. For that you have to go look, the way we will in the next four chapters, and you have to ask what the comparison group received.

Those two paragraphs are the same paragraph, said to two different people. The position of a practice in the medical system and the quality of the evidence behind it are separate questions, and this chapter exists so that you will never again mistake one for the other.

Where the evidence runs out

Every chapter has this section, and in this chapter it needs to be longer than usual, because the limits here are more serious than anywhere else in the book.

First and most importantly: this is the chapter where my own competence is thinnest. I am a clinician with training in pharmacology, not a historian of medicine, and the material in this chapter comes from secondary reading rather than from archives. I have tried to confine myself to the broad shape that nobody seriously disputes: that there were many competing sects, that educational reform and licensure reorganized the field in the early twentieth century, that the Flexner Report existed and had the general effect described, and that the sects largely lost their institutions. Where a specific number would have made a sentence more impressive, I have written the sentence without it, on purpose, and you should notice how often that happened.

Second: Starr. The central claim I attributed to him, that American physicians won cultural authority and not merely prosperity, is how his argument is commonly summarized, and I have flagged it in our fact base as needing verification against his actual text. That is why there is not a single quotation from him in this chapter. Treat the whole section on sovereignty as a well regarded interpretation that I have handed you secondhand. If you write about it in your assignment, go read the book rather than trusting me, and tell me if I have it wrong. Students who have checked their instructor's sources are the students I most enjoy teaching.

Third, and this one is unfixable: the counterfactual is unknowable. We cannot know what American health would look like had the medical marketplace stayed open. There was no control group. No state ran the alternative for eighty years so we could compare. Anyone who tells you that we would be healthier today with a pluralistic system, or that we would be dying of fevers without the consolidation, is telling you about their politics. The honest position is that this is a single historical path with no comparison, and in a book that keeps insisting on asking what the comparison group received, we have to admit when there is no comparison group at all.

Fourth: the weighting. How much of the sects' decline was force one, how much force two, how much force three, how much force four? Historians genuinely disagree, and the disagreement does not look close to resolution, partly because the forces are entangled in the way I described and partly because the question may not have a determinate answer. I have given you the four and a method for detecting each. I have deliberately not given you proportions, because I do not have them and nobody does.

Fifth: motive. I argued above that the private motives of the reformers are mostly unrecoverable and that we should judge consequences instead. I want to flag that this is a choice on my part, not a settled method. A historian might reasonably say that intentions matter morally and that correspondence and committee records give real access to them. I find mixed motives so normal that I distrust accounts that sort historical actors into reformers and schemers. But that is my disposition, and you should know it is a disposition and not a finding.

Sixth: what the antitrust finding does and does not settle. It establishes documented organized conduct in a particular period against a particular profession. It does not establish a general pattern of suppression across traditions or centuries, and it says nothing whatever about whether any treatment works. I have seen that finding stretched to cover both, by people in my own field, and the stretch does not hold.

Seventh, and this is the one I find most interesting: I do not know whether homeopathy declined because its central claim was refuted or because its competitor stopped doing harm and thereby destroyed its advantage. Those are different explanations with different lessons, both have serious people defending them, and the available evidence does not cleanly separate them. I raise it not to score a point against homeopathy or for it, but because it is the cleanest illustration in the whole book of how much depends on what a thing is being compared to.

What I am confident of, and will assert, is narrower than what this chapter covers. The marketplace was genuinely crowded. The reorganization happened largely through education and licensure rather than through clinical trials. The central institutional victory preceded the great therapeutic advances. And a third of American adults are currently using the traditions that supposedly lost. Everything past that is interpretation, mine included.

  • Can you state Starr's argument about cultural authority in your own words, say what it would mean in a clinic today, and explain why it is an argument rather than a settled finding?

The Hands On Traditions

Can a tradition be right about what helps and wrong about why it helps, and what should we do with it if so?

A note on who is writing this chapter

In chapter 1 I told you there would be one chapter in this book you should read harder than the others. This is that chapter, and I want to make the reason unmistakable before you read another paragraph.

I am a licensed Doctor of Chiropractic. I hold an active license, I have held one for years, and I adjust patients most days of the week. The income that pays my mortgage comes partly from the profession this chapter examines. I trained inside it. My classmates are my friends. The people who taught me to feel a spine are people I admire. When I write the word adjustment, I am not writing about something I read about in a journal. I am writing about something my hands know how to do, and have done thousands of times, and which I believe has helped a great many people.

That is a conflict of interest, and I want to be precise about what kind. The obvious kind is money, and the money is real, but money is the smaller half. The larger half is identity. A person who has spent a decade becoming competent at something, who has been thanked for it, who introduces himself at parties by naming it, is not a neutral reader of the evidence about it. He will find the supportive studies more convincing than a stranger would. He will remember the patient who walked out standing straighter and forget the three who quietly stopped scheduling. None of that requires dishonesty. Motivated reasoning is not lying. It works on careful, decent people, which is what makes it dangerous, and the only known defense is for somebody else to check your work.

So here is what I am asking of you, and it is a real request, not a throat clearing gesture toward humility. Read this chapter with a pen. When I describe chiropractic, ask whether I would have described ayurveda that way. When you get to chapter 5 and I take apart a tradition I do not practice, come back here and compare the temperature of the two chapters. If chapter 3 is warmer, softer, hedged in my favor, full of maybes where chapter 5 has flatly nots, then I have failed at the one thing this book is actually for, and you should say so in class. I would rather be caught by a first year student than be wrong in print for seven weeks.

I should name the opposite failure too, because it is just as available to me and harder to spot. A practitioner who wants to look credible can overcorrect. He can be harder on his own field than the evidence warrants and purchase your trust with it. That is not honesty either. It is a different way of making the chapter about me instead of the subject. I have tried to land on the truth rather than either performance, and the truth, as I read it, is genuinely mixed in a way that will satisfy neither the practitioner who wants vindication nor the skeptic who wants a scalp.

I have read a great deal about yoga and I do not teach it, and I will tell you when that is the case, because implying practitioner level familiarity I do not have would be its own kind of fraud. This chapter is different. Here I know the inside. I know what gets taught in the second year of a chiropractic program and what gets said in the hallway afterward. I know what a treatment plan looks like when the billing calendar shaped it rather than the patient. That knowledge is worth something to you, and it is also exactly what makes me partisan. Use it and discount it.

Chiropractic, osteopathy and massage, in their own words

This chapter groups three traditions together because they share a tool. In all three, the treatment is a pair of hands on a body. That is the only thing they reliably share. Their origins are different, their theories are different, their legal status is different, and in one case the same word names two entirely different professions depending on which country you are standing in. Grouping them under the heading hands on is a convenience for a syllabus, and it is also the first example of this chapter's second big theme, which is what happens when one word is asked to carry more than one job.

We take each one in its own vocabulary first, at its strongest, before any evaluation. If my summary of massage would make a massage therapist wince, I am not ready to assess massage.

Chiropractic as chiropractic states it

Chiropractic began in Davenport, Iowa, in 1895, with a man named D.D. Palmer and a patient named Harvey Lillard. Palmer was a magnetic healer, which was then a recognizable occupation, and Lillard was a janitor who had lost much of his hearing. The founding story is that Palmer found a displaced vertebra in Lillard's back, pushed it into place, and Lillard's hearing improved. Whether the story is literally true is argued about. What matters for us is the inference Palmer drew from it, because that inference became a profession.

The inference was this. The nervous system coordinates everything the body does. Nerves exit the spinal column between the vertebrae. If a vertebra sits or moves wrongly, it interferes with the nerves passing by it, and whatever those nerves supply will function badly. Palmer called the misaligned segment a vertebral subluxation. Correct the subluxation and you remove the interference. You do not have to heal the patient, because the patient heals himself. You have to get out of his way.

That last sentence is the philosophical heart of the tradition. Palmer wrote about innate intelligence, an organizing capacity in the living body that directs healing and growth without conscious instruction. In this framework the body is not a machine to be repaired with substances. It is a self regulating system with a built in tendency toward health, and disease is what happens when that tendency is obstructed. The doctor's job is to find the obstruction and remove it. This is a position philosophers of medicine call vitalism, and it explains a great deal about early chiropractic that otherwise looks merely stubborn, including its hostility to drugs and surgery. If healing comes from inside, adding chemicals from outside is at best irrelevant and at worst another obstruction.

The treatment itself is a specific physical act. The classical form is a high velocity low amplitude thrust: the practitioner positions a joint near the end of its available range, then delivers a short, fast, shallow push along a chosen line. It is fast rather than forceful. The audible pop is generally understood to be gas coming out of solution inside the joint, not bone moving against bone, and its presence or absence is not a measure of whether anything useful happened, though patients and some practitioners treat it that way. There are also lower force methods, instrument assisted thrusts, drop table techniques and slow sustained pressures, chosen by patient size, age, bone quality and tolerance.

Now the part students are rarely told, which is that the profession I have just described is not the profession most chiropractors practice in today. For most of a century chiropractic has contained two parties, historically called straights and mixers, and the argument between them is real and sometimes bitter. The straight position holds that correcting vertebral subluxation is the whole of chiropractic, and that diluting it with physical therapy or a pain management framing is a betrayal. The mixer position holds that chiropractic is a health profession that uses manual treatment of the spine among other tools, and should be judged by patient outcomes like any other. A large part of the modern profession has moved further still, and would describe itself as a conservative spine care profession whose business is musculoskeletal pain, with no particular commitment to Palmer's physiology at all.

So when someone asks what chiropractic claims, the honest answer is that it depends which chiropractor you ask, and a field that cannot agree on its own central claim is in a poor position to defend it.

Let me state the modern mainstream version at its strongest, because this is the version that has to be taken seriously. A patient arrives with ordinary mechanical low back pain. She gets a full history and a physical examination that includes watching her move. She gets screened for the small number of serious conditions that can hide behind back pain. She is told, in plain words, what is probably going on and what is probably not. She receives hands on treatment, is shown how to move, and is encouraged to stay active rather than rest. She leaves without a prescription, without an image she did not need, and with a follow up appointment. That package is close to what current thinking about back pain recommends, and it is more than a great deal of hurried primary care manages to deliver. That is a real service, and it is worth saying so before we start taking things apart.

One more piece of history belongs here, because it affects how you read the profession's marginal position. Chiropractic did not simply lose an argument about evidence and retreat to the edges of American health care. In 1987 a federal court found that the American Medical Association had engaged in an unlawful conspiracy to contain and eliminate the chiropractic profession, in the case usually cited as Wilk v. American Medical Association. That finding does not make Palmer right about nerve interference. It is not evidence about whether adjustments help. But it does mean that part of why chiropractic developed outside mainstream medicine is organized exclusion rather than scientific judgment, and a student who does not know that will misread the whole history.

Osteopathy as osteopathy states it

Osteopathy began in the American Midwest in the nineteenth century with a physician named Andrew Taylor Still. Still had practiced conventional medicine of his era, had lost children to infectious disease, and had come to regard the drug therapy available to him as useless at best and poisonous often. He was not wrong about the drug therapy of his era, which is a point worth pausing on. Much of the nineteenth century reaction against mainstream medicine was a reasonable response to a mainstream medicine that was genuinely dangerous.

Still's alternative rested on principles that modern osteopathic education still teaches in recognizable form. The body is a unit, of body, mind and spirit. It has self regulating and self healing mechanisms. Structure and function are reciprocally related, meaning that how a body is arranged governs how it works, and how it works reshapes how it is built. Rational treatment is based on those three principles together. Still held that much illness could be traced to disorders of the musculoskeletal frame interfering with circulation and nerve supply, and that correcting the frame by hand would let the body's own machinery do the rest.

Notice how much of that overlaps with Palmer, and how it also differs. Both are vitalist, both locate the problem in structure, both make the practitioner a remover of obstruction rather than an administrator of substances. But Still's lesion was never only about vertebrae and never only about nerves. He thought about blood supply and lymphatic drainage as much as nerve transmission, and about the whole frame rather than a specific misaligned segment. His osteopathic lesion became, in modern terminology, somatic dysfunction: impaired or altered function of the components of the body framework, meaning the bony, joint, muscular, fascial and related vascular, lymphatic and neural elements, considered together.

The osteopathic toolkit is correspondingly broader than the classical chiropractic one. It includes the same high velocity low amplitude thrust, but it also includes muscle energy technique, in which the patient contracts a muscle against the practitioner's resistance so that the practitioner can then take the joint further; counterstrain, in which a tender point is held in the position of greatest comfort until it quiets; myofascial release, which is a sustained stretch of the sheets of connective tissue that wrap muscle; lymphatic pump techniques; and cranial osteopathy, which I will come back to because it is the weakest limb of the tradition and I am not going to skip it.

Then comes the part that makes osteopathy the single most instructive case in this chapter. In the United States, osteopathy did not stay outside. Osteopathic colleges added the basic sciences, added hospital training, added residencies, and American osteopaths became fully licensed physicians. A Doctor of Osteopathic Medicine in the United States completes medical school and residency, prescribes drugs, performs surgery, and practices in every specialty. In the United States, the word osteopath names a physician.

Outside the United States, in the United Kingdom and much of Europe and Australia, osteopaths are regulated manual therapy practitioners who are not physicians, do not prescribe, and do not do surgery. The two groups share a founder and a vocabulary and almost nothing else about their legal standing or daily work. Hold on to this. It is going to be the cleanest example in the next section of a single word doing two jobs.

There is a cost to the American story that the profession does not always acknowledge. As osteopathic medicine integrated, osteopathic manipulative treatment became less central to it. The more completely the profession was absorbed, the less distinctive its signature intervention became in practice. A tradition can win by being absorbed, and the price of absorption is usually its distinctive mechanism. Willow bark became aspirin, and in becoming aspirin it stopped being an herbal tradition.

Now cranial osteopathy, since I promised. The claim is that the bones of the skull retain a small rhythmic motion, that this motion relates to the circulation of cerebrospinal fluid, that a trained hand can feel it, and that gentle pressure can correct restrictions in it, with effects reaching well beyond the head. See the logical structure here rather than the plausibility, because the structure is where this falls down first. Before you can ask whether correcting a cranial restriction helps anybody, you have to establish that the restriction can be detected reliably, which means that two trained examiners feeling the same head should agree about what they find. If they do not agree, then whatever is guiding the treatment is not a property of the patient. The reliability work that exists is not encouraging. I am not going to hand you a number, because the fact base for this book does not contain one I have read and checked myself. But of everything in this chapter, cranial technique has the furthest to go before its central finding has been shown to exist.

Therapeutic massage as massage states it

Massage is the oldest of the three and the least theorized, and those two facts are related. Nobody founded massage. There is no Palmer and no Still, no founding patient, no originating doctrine to defend or abandon. What there is instead is a set of lineages that developed separately and have partly merged: the European tradition of systematic manual treatment that reaches American practice as Swedish massage, the Chinese bodywork tradition of tui na, the Japanese finger pressure tradition of shiatsu, the Hawaiian lomilomi, and a crowd of twentieth century additions including trigger point and deep tissue work.

The claim, stated plainly and at its strongest, is that skilled manipulation of soft tissue reduces pain and muscle tension, improves the ease and range of movement, lowers anxiety, improves sleep, and improves a person's general sense of well being. Notice how modest that claim is compared with the two we have just looked at. Massage does not traditionally claim to cure disease by removing an obstruction to an organizing intelligence. It claims to make bodies hurt less and feel better. That modesty matters enormously, because the more modest the claim, the less work the evidence has to do.

The mechanism stories offered for massage are where the trouble is. You will hear that it increases blood flow, that it moves lymph, that it breaks up adhesions between tissue layers, that it releases trigger points, that it reduces sympathetic nervous system arousal, and that it flushes toxins out of the muscles. These are not equally serious proposals. The reduction in arousal is at least the sort of thing that can be measured, and a reasonable person could expect it from any sustained, safe, predictable touch. The adhesion story makes a specific anatomical claim and asks you to accept that a hand can do that to tissue through skin and fat at the forces involved. And the toxin story is not a mechanism at all.

I want to be careful here, because it would be easy to make this sound like a dismissal of massage and it is not. The toxin story is a good teaching example precisely because the practice around it may be perfectly fine. Ask three questions of anyone who tells you massage flushes toxins. Which toxin. How is it measured. What was the level before and after. If the answers do not come, and they almost never do, then the word toxin is not naming anything. It is naming a feeling, the feeling of something bad leaving. That is a real feeling and may well be part of why people walk out lighter than they walked in. But it is not an account of what happened to the tissue, and a student who can tell an explanation from a mood has learned something this course exists to teach.

Massage therapy is regulated by state license across much of the United States, with a scope that generally covers soft tissue work and generally does not include diagnosing conditions or performing joint manipulation. The details vary considerably by state, so read your own state's statute rather than trust a textbook, including this one.

Claim, mechanism, evidence, worked through

Now we run the machinery from chapter 1 on my own field, carefully, in order, on the best case available to it. Not on colic, not on asthma, not on immune function. On ordinary mechanical low back pain in an adult, which is what the overwhelming majority of chiropractic patients actually come in with. Recall from chapter 1 that of adults using chiropractic care in 2022, 85.7 percent were using it for pain management, the highest share of any of the seven approaches that survey tracked. If chiropractic is going to be assessed on what it mostly does, this is what it mostly does.

Meet Marcus. He is 44, he loads trucks, and nine days ago he bent sideways to reach a box and felt something let go in his low back. He can walk but not comfortably. Sitting is the worst. He has no fever, no weakness in his legs, no change in bowel or bladder function, no history of cancer, no trauma beyond the box. He has taken what was in the cabinet and it took the edge off. He is frightened, mostly because his uncle had back surgery and never came back to work. He books with a chiropractor because his coworker swears by one.

Question one. What is the claim? Stated the way a practitioner would state it: hands on treatment of Marcus's spine, delivered over a short series of visits, will reduce his pain and improve his function faster and further than leaving him alone would.

That is the claim at a defensible strength. Notice what it does not say. It does not say Marcus's back is out of place. It does not say anything about nerve interference. It does not promise a cure, a permanent fix, or an effect on anything other than his back. Many practitioners would state something stronger. We will test the stronger versions in a moment. But this is the claim we can examine most usefully, and it is the claim the mainstream of my profession would put forward.

Question two. What is the proposed mechanism? Here is where this chapter earns its place in the book, because there is not one answer. There are at least four, they are commonly blurred together in conversation, and they are not variations on a theme. They are incompatible accounts that predict different things.

The first is the classical account. Marcus has a vertebral subluxation. It is interfering with nerve function. The adjustment removes the interference and his own healing capacity does the rest.

The second is biomechanical. A spinal joint has become restricted in its movement. The thrust restores motion to that joint. The improvement is mechanical: a stuck thing is now unstuck.

The third is neurophysiological. The thrust delivers a large, brief burst of input from the sensors in the joint capsule, muscle and skin into the spinal cord. That barrage changes muscle tone locally and changes how the nervous system is processing pain signals more centrally. The joint may not need to have moved anywhere in particular. What mattered was the input.

The fourth account says the specifically manual part of this is doing little or none of the work. What helped Marcus was being examined by someone who took him seriously, being told his back was not broken, being touched firmly and safely by a confident person, being given permission to move, expecting to improve, and the simple passage of nine more days.

Stop and look at what those four predict, because this is the most useful thinking you will do in this chapter. If the first is right, the level matters enormously, the finding should be identifiable, and adjusting Marcus's spine is a reasonable approach to conditions that have nothing to do with his back, because the nerves go everywhere. If the second is right, the restricted joint should be findable, two examiners should agree on which one it is, and motion testing should show the before and after. If the third is right, precision of segment barely matters, and the idea of a specific lesion to be corrected becomes decoration. If the fourth is right, a convincing fake thrust should work about as well as a real one, and Marcus's improvement tells us about care and time rather than about spines.

These are not academic distinctions. They are a menu of experiments. And the uncomfortable fact for my profession is that the four accounts are often used interchangeably by the same practitioner in the same conversation, sliding to whichever one the listener seems likely to accept. I have watched it happen.

Question three. What does the evidence show, and compared with what?

The honest summary, stated qualitatively, is this. For ordinary non specific low back pain, meaning back pain without an identified serious cause, manual therapy of the spine sits inside the set of reasonable first options. The benefits that studies find are generally modest. The literature is contested, and reviews of it have reached meaningfully different conclusions depending on which trials they included and which comparison they treated as the main question. That is the state of play as I read it, and you should notice that there is not a single number in that paragraph.

I have left the numbers out on purpose, and that is a methodological decision rather than evasion. Every factual claim in this book traces to a checked source file, and the manual therapy effect sizes are not in it in a form I have personally read and verified. A precise number that I half remember would be more memorable than the literature deserves and would teach you false confidence, which is the specific failure mode this course is designed to inoculate you against. If you want the number, go find the current systematic reviews, read the certainty ratings alongside the estimates, and see how wide the uncertainty is. That is not me ducking the assignment. That is the assignment.

But I can tell you precisely why the evidence here is harder to pin down than the evidence for acupuncture or meditation, and the reasons are more instructive than any effect size would be.

The first reason is that manipulation is not one intervention. Trials labeled spinal manipulation have delivered thrusts, mobilizations, which are slow rhythmic movements rather than fast thrusts, instrument assisted taps, soft tissue work, and combinations of all of these, over anywhere from one visit to a dozen, by chiropractors, osteopaths and physical therapists trained differently. When a review pools those trials, the number it produces is an average across procedures practitioners would not consider equivalent. Compare a drug trial, where the intervention is ten milligrams of a specified molecule.

The second reason is the sham problem, and it is severe. A sham is a fake version of a treatment, built to be indistinguishable from the real thing to the patient, so that researchers can separate the effect of the treatment itself from the effect of receiving treatment. For a pill this is easy: make one that looks identical and contains nothing. For a thrust that takes a joint to the end of its range, produces a loud pop, and leaves the patient feeling distinctly that something happened, there is no good fake. Every attempted sham for manipulation is either obviously not a real adjustment, in which case the patient can tell and the blinding has failed, or close enough to a real one that it might itself be therapeutic, in which case you are comparing a treatment with a treatment and calling one of them nothing.

You have seen this problem elsewhere in the course already. The Cochrane review of yoga for chronic non specific low back pain, which we come to properly in chapter 4, downgraded its certainty partly because no trial compared yoga with sham yoga and because participants always knew which group they were in. That is the same structural limitation, written up by careful reviewers about a different tradition. It is not special pleading on behalf of chiropractic. It is what happens to any treatment a patient can feel.

The third reason is the natural history of back pain. Most episodes of ordinary low back pain improve substantially without any treatment at all. I am deliberately not giving you a percentage or a timeline, because the honest ranges are wide and depend on how you define an episode. But the direction is not controversial, and it has a brutal implication. Marcus books an appointment on the worst day, which is exactly when people book appointments. He would have been better in a week whatever anyone did. His improvement after treatment is therefore not evidence that the treatment worked, and neither is the improvement of the next thousand patients, which is why practitioner experience, including mine, is a poor guide here. Statisticians call this regression to the mean: measure anything at its extreme and the next measurement will tend to be less extreme, treatment or no treatment. It is the most common way a sincere clinician comes to believe something false, and the only defense is a comparison group.

Which brings us back to the third question's last three words: compared with what.

I am going to show you the shape of the answer using numbers from a different tradition, because those numbers are checked and because the shape is the lesson. The largest analysis of acupuncture for chronic pain, which pooled the individual patient records from 39 trials covering 20,827 patients, found that patients who received acupuncture did better than patients who received no acupuncture by roughly half a standard deviation, and better than patients who received sham acupuncture by roughly two tenths of a standard deviation. A standard deviation is just a yardstick for how spread out a set of measurements is, so half a standard deviation is a difference a patient would notice. Two tenths is much smaller, and it is the part attributable to where the needles went. Both numbers come from the same analysis. The authors concluded acupuncture is effective for chronic pain, and also reported that effect size varied mainly with what the control group received rather than with the acupuncture technique used.

Sit with that gap between the half and the two tenths, because it is the most useful single fact in this book. Most of what the acupuncture patient feels is real. Most of it is not specific to the needles. Both halves are true at once, and anyone who quotes one number without the other is telling you half a story, whichever half they picked.

I am citing acupuncture numbers in a chapter about manipulation deliberately, and I want to be exact about what I am and am not claiming. I am not telling you that spinal manipulation has been shown to have the same split. We do not have that result, for the reason I gave: we cannot build the sham that would produce it. What I will tell you is my expectation, labeled clearly as an expectation and not a finding, because you are entitled to hold me to it. I expect that if we could run that study for manipulation, the shape would look broadly similar. A substantial benefit relative to doing nothing. A much smaller portion of it attributable to the specific thing my hands do. That result would make most of the theory I was taught decorative and most of the clinical value real, and I think I could live with it.

Right about what, wrong about why

A tradition can be right about what helps while being wrong about why it helps. These are separate claims. They are supported by separate kinds of evidence. They deserve separate verdicts. And the single most common error in writing about holistic health, made with equal enthusiasm by enthusiasts and by skeptics, is to treat them as one claim so that a verdict on either settles both.

The enthusiast version runs: patients get better, therefore the mechanism is correct. Marcus walked out standing straighter, therefore his subluxation was corrected. That does not follow. Marcus improving tells you something happened. It does not tell you which of four incompatible accounts of what happened is the right one, and it certainly does not confirm the account the practitioner happens to favor.

The skeptic version runs: the mechanism is nonsense, therefore the treatment does nothing. That does not follow either, and it is the more interesting error because it is held by people who consider themselves careful. Palmer's physiology can be wrong in every particular, and Marcus can still get up off the table in less pain than he lay down in. The quality of a mechanism story and the size of a clinical effect are not the same variable. Medicine knows this about its own history. Willow bark reduced fever and pain for centuries before anybody had heard of a prostaglandin. The bark did not start working when the chemistry was understood. It started being understood.

So far this sounds like good news for my field. Here is the part that is not, and it is the hinge of the chapter.

A wrong mechanism is not harmless, because mechanism is what licenses extrapolation. The whole point of knowing why something works is that it tells you what else to try. If aspirin works by inhibiting an enzyme, you know something about which other conditions it might touch and which drugs it will interact with. And if you have the mechanism wrong, your extrapolations go wrong in a specific, predictable direction: you will confidently apply the treatment to conditions it cannot reach.

That is not a hypothetical for chiropractic. It is the profession's most serious failure, and it descends directly from the founding mechanism. If subluxation interferes with nerve transmission, and nerves supply every organ, then adjusting the spine is a rational approach to asthma, to ear infections in children, to infant colic, to high blood pressure, to almost anything. The logic is clean. It follows from the premise. And the claims for those conditions have not been supported, there is no mechanism for them that survives examination, and when a parent brings an infant for adjustments instead of to a pediatrician, the cost is not theoretical. It is the same pattern chapter 1 identified as the real source of harm in this entire field: not the toxicity of the alternative treatment, but the conventional care that was declined.

Meet the other patient. Dana brings her nine week old in because he cries inconsolably in the evenings and she has not slept properly in a month. A chiropractor who believes the classical mechanism has something to offer her. A chiropractor who believes the neurophysiological account has very little to offer her, should say so, and should ask whether the baby has been weighed recently and whether she has anyone helping her at night. The mechanism the practitioner holds determines what happens in that room. That is why getting it wrong matters even when the treatment is gentle.

So the error is not having an unproven mechanism. Every young field has one. Osteopathy had one, massage has several, and conventional medicine has operated for long stretches on physiology it later discarded. The error is acting on it as though it were established, past the border of where the clinical results are. Hold both halves. The clinical effect in mechanical back pain deserves to be taken seriously. The founding theory does not get to ride along on it into the chest and the middle ear.

One word, several meanings

There is a specific move that makes bad arguments in this field look strong, and once you can see it you will see it constantly. It has a name. Philosophers call it equivocation, and it works like this: a word with two meanings appears twice in an argument, the first time in the meaning that makes the premise true, the second time in the meaning the conclusion needs. Each step looks fine. The argument as a whole proves nothing. And because the word never changed its spelling, almost nobody notices that the ground moved.

The cleanest example is in my own field, and it involves its most important word. Subluxation has two meanings. In orthopedic medicine it means a partial dislocation: a joint whose surfaces have lost their normal relationship but have not fully separated. That is a real, serious, visible thing. It appears in every orthopedic textbook, it shows up on imaging, and it gets treated. In chiropractic the same word names a spinal segment with altered position or movement that is said to interfere with nerve function, usually not visible on any image, often identified by palpation, and not the same entity at all.

Now watch the argument. Subluxations are a well established orthopedic phenomenon, documented in the medical literature for a century. Chiropractic is the profession that specializes in detecting and correcting subluxations. Therefore chiropractic rests on well established medical science.

Every sentence there is defensible on one reading. The conclusion is worthless. The first sentence is true of the orthopedic meaning. The second describes the chiropractic meaning. Nothing has been established about the chiropractic subluxation at all, and the argument gets its persuasive force from the reader not noticing that one word did two jobs. Register that I have just run this on my own profession, with its central term, and that this argument appears in print and in practice marketing. There is a live argument inside chiropractic about whether to keep the word, and I think that argument is about this equivocation, whether or not the people having it put it that way.

The second example is osteopathy, and it is useful because it cuts in both directions.

Pro direction: osteopathic physicians in the United States complete medical school and residency training, prescribe medication, and practice in every specialty of medicine. Osteopathic manipulative treatment is what osteopathy is built on. Therefore osteopathic manipulation is a part of mainstream medical practice. Again, true premises, worthless conclusion. The first sentence is about American physicians. The second is about a technique. The integration of a profession is not evidence about the efficacy of its signature procedure.

Critical direction, and this one gets made just as often: osteopathy is founded on the osteopathic lesion, a nineteenth century idea that structural disorders of the frame cause disease throughout the body, which has not held up. Your cardiologist is an osteopath. Therefore your cardiologist is practicing discredited nineteenth century medicine. Identical structure, opposite agenda, equally empty. The word osteopath named a non physician manual practitioner in one premise and a board certified physician in the other.

Third example, which you met in chapter 1. Works. Three meanings, at least. Patients feel better after it. It is better than nothing. It is better than the best available alternative. A sentence like massage works for back pain is true on the first meaning, probably true on the second, and considerably harder to defend on the third. When somebody tells you a treatment works, you have not been told anything until you know which of the three they mean, and usually they do not know themselves.

Fourth, and this one is practical. In careful usage, manipulation involves a thrust past the end of the joint's active range, mobilization does not, and adjustment is the chiropractic term for a manipulation delivered with a specific intent. In advertising, in conversation, and sometimes in research write ups, the three get used interchangeably. So when you read that a review found a benefit for manual therapy, ask what the practitioners in those trials actually did with their hands. The honest answer will often be several different things.

Fifth. Natural. Massage is natural, therefore massage is safe. Natural here means not manufactured, which is true, and is then used to mean harmless, which is a different claim requiring separate support. Lead is natural. Chapter 5 will introduce you to a study of 70 ayurvedic herbal products bought from South Asian grocery stores in the Boston area, 14 of which, one in five, contained lead, mercury or arsenic.

Here is the habit I want you to build out of all of this, and it is almost mechanical. When an argument in this field feels stronger than the sum of its parts, go looking for the word that changed jobs in the middle. Then ask the person making the argument to restate it without using that word at all, substituting a definition each time. If the argument survives, it was a good argument and the word was just shorthand. If it evaporates, the word was doing the work, and the work it was doing was hiding a gap.

And run it on the critics, because the move is not partisan. Chiropractic is unscientific sounds like one claim. It is at least three. It can mean the founding theory is incompatible with what we know about physiology, which is largely fair. It can mean the treatment does not help patients, which is a separate empirical question with a more favorable answer. It can mean practitioners do not use or produce research, which is a claim about a profession's culture. A critic who slides between those three has not refuted anything either.

What the reviews actually say

Now both cases, as strongly as I can make them, because a chapter about my own field that produced only one of them would be exactly the failure I warned you about on the first page. Start with the strongest case for manual therapy. Not the version a practice website would run, the version I think actually holds.

First, it is in the right competitive set, and this is the argument most critics skip. The question is never whether manipulation is better than a perfect treatment. The question is what Marcus should do on day nine. His realistic options are medication with real risks, imaging that will probably find something incidental and frighten him about it, injections, a surgical pathway with its own uneven evidence, or nothing at all while he stays scared and stops moving. Against that set, a treatment whose honest verdict is modest benefit and favorable safety in the low back is not an embarrassment. Parity with exercise, if that is where the evidence lands, is parity with roughly the best thing we have.

Second, the package delivers things that are therapeutic whatever the mechanism turns out to be. Marcus gets examined. He gets screened for the dangerous causes. He gets told, by someone who touched his back and seemed to know what he was feeling, that his spine is not damaged and that he should move. Chapter 1's point about nonspecific effects applies with full force: nonspecific does not mean fake. A nonspecific effect is still a reduction in suffering.

Third, people are voting with their feet in enormous numbers, and that is data about something even if it is not data about mechanism. Of adults using chiropractic care in 2022, 85.7 percent were using it for pain management, with a confidence interval from 84.2 to 87.2, the highest proportion among the seven approaches tracked. Overall use of complementary approaches went from 19.2 percent of United States adults in 2002 to 36.7 percent in 2022. These professions are absorbing an immense amount of real demand for help with pain that the rest of the system was not meeting.

Now the strongest case against, in the words a serious critic would use rather than a softened paraphrase.

First, the founding mechanism is unsupported and it is still taught and still sold. Not by everyone, not everywhere, but in enough places that a patient walking into an unfamiliar office cannot know which profession she is getting. A field that has not retired a central claim it cannot support has a standards problem, and the fact that many individual practitioners have personally moved on does not fix it, because the patient is not choosing a practitioner's private beliefs. She is choosing a sign on a door.

Second, because the mechanism is unsupported, the profession has no principled brake on scope. If you cannot say precisely what the adjustment does, you cannot say precisely what it does not do, and the claims drift outward into conditions where the evidence is absent and the opportunity cost is real. This is not an occasional excess at the fringe. It is a predictable consequence of the theory.

Third, the economics are built wrong, and I will say this from the inside because I have seen it. A model that bills per visit rewards frequency. A treatment plan of three visits a week for six weeks, justified by a finding that cannot be independently verified, is a conflict of interest embedded in the structure of the encounter rather than in the character of the practitioner. Good people practice inside bad incentives all the time and do not notice the slope they are on. The design of the thing does not protect patients, and a serious profession would redesign it.

Fourth, the specificity claim is in trouble before the trials even start. The classical and biomechanical accounts both require that a practitioner can identify which segment needs treating, which requires that two trained practitioners examining the same spine agree about which segment it is. The reliability literature on manual spinal assessment is not reassuring, and I am again declining to give you a number for the same reason as before. But notice the logic: if the finding cannot be reliably detected, then whatever is guiding the choice of level comes from the practitioner rather than the patient, and the specificity story is finished regardless of whether the treatment helps.

Fifth, my profession has sometimes treated the sham problem as a defense rather than a limitation. The argument runs that because manipulation cannot be properly blinded, it cannot be held to the standard other treatments are held to. That is backwards. An unanswerable question is not an answered one. The correct conclusion from an unbuildable sham is that we do not know how much of the effect is specific, and should stop making claims that require the answer to be high.

Sixth, and this is the one I find hardest to write. There is a visible minority within chiropractic that is hostile to vaccination and to other public health measures, and this is not a caricature manufactured by critics. It descends with depressing logic from the founding philosophy, since if health comes from within and disease is obstruction, then introducing a biological agent from outside looks like an obstruction rather than a protection. It does real harm, it harms children in particular, and it is, in my judgment, the single greatest discredit to a profession I otherwise defend. A colleague who advises a parent against a childhood vaccine is doing something indefensible, and I would rather say so in a textbook than pretend the problem is small.

Seventh is cervical manipulation and arterial injury, which belongs in the next section because the honest answer is that it is not settled.

Now the comparison structure that decides between the two cases, using the two places in this course where the comparison has been done properly.

The meditation literature gives the cleanest example of a reversal. A review of 47 randomized trials covering 3,515 participants, deliberately restricted to trials with active control groups so that the effect of simply receiving attention could be separated out, found moderate evidence that mindfulness meditation programs improve anxiety, with an effect size of 0.38 at eight weeks falling to 0.22 at three to six months, depression at 0.30 and then 0.23, and pain at 0.33. The same review found no evidence that meditation programs were better than any active treatment, such as drugs, exercise or other behavioral therapies. Both findings are in the same paper. Meditation beats nothing. Meditation has not been shown to beat going for a run.

The yoga literature gives the same shape for a condition closer to home. The Cochrane review of yoga for chronic non specific low back pain concluded that yoga is probably better than no exercise for pain and back related function at three months, with small improvements, and that there is probably little or no difference in back related function compared with other back focused exercise, with the evidence on pain for that comparison uncertain. Certainty was downgraded to moderate, low or very low, for the reasons we discussed: no sham, unblinded participants, small trials, inconsistent results.

That shape, better than nothing and not clearly better than other active care, is a common finding in this field, and I will tell you plainly that it is the shape I expect for manual therapy in low back pain. Label that as what it is: my expectation, not a result I am citing. If the eventual answer is that shape, then the enthusiast who claimed chiropractic is uniquely effective was wrong, the skeptic who claimed it does nothing was also wrong, and the useful conclusion is the boring one. It is one reasonable option among several for a common problem, and the choice should turn on access, cost, patient preference and risk rather than on anybody's theory of healing.

Where the evidence runs out

Four honest gaps, and one note about how I handled history.

The first gap is the one I keep returning to, so I will state it as a conclusion rather than an aside. The specificity question for spinal manipulation may be permanently unanswerable with the methods we have. We cannot build a sham thrust a patient cannot detect, and without one we cannot do for manipulation what the acupuncture collaboration did for needling, which was to split the total benefit into the part that came from the procedure and the part that came from receiving care. I would very much like that number to exist. I think there is a real chance it never will, and I would rather tell you that than imply the gap is a temporary research funding problem.

The second gap is safety at the neck, and this is the most important paragraph in the chapter for anyone who will work in or refer to this field, so read it slowly.

There is a documented association between manipulation of the cervical spine and dissection of the arteries that run up through the neck to the brain, which is a tear in the artery wall that can lead to a stroke. The association appears in case reports and in studies comparing people who had strokes with people who did not. What is genuinely contested is the direction of causation, and the reason it is contested is clinically important rather than merely technical. The early symptoms of a dissection that is already in progress are neck pain and headache. Neck pain and headache are the most common reasons a person walks into a chiropractic office. So the patient may be arriving at my office because of a dissection that has already begun rather than leaving it with one, and some portion of the reported association is probably that. How large a portion, nobody can currently say.

I am not going to give you a risk figure, and that is a deliberate refusal rather than an oversight. The estimates in circulation differ by orders of magnitude depending on what denominator is used, how cases were ascertained, and whether the study design could separate the two causal stories above. There is no comprehensive reporting system that would settle it, and a safety number is the last place I would guess. What I will tell you is what I do, because clinical practice does not get to wait for certainty. I ask specifically about sudden onset headache or neck pain unlike anything the patient has had before, about visual changes, about dizziness. I treat the possibility as real even though I cannot quantify it, and I use lower force approaches or decline to treat when something in the history is unusual. And here is how to assess what you hear from others. If a chiropractor tells you the risk is zero, he is not reading the literature. If a critic tells you it is common, neither is he. The best available answer is that serious events appear to be rare and that nobody can responsibly tell you how rare.

The third gap concerns the claims for conditions beyond the musculoskeletal system, and here two things often get merged. Whether spinal manipulation treats asthma or ear infections through nerve interference is not really an open question: the claims have not been supported and the proposed mechanism does not survive examination. But a narrower question genuinely is open, because indirect routes exist. A person whose chronic back pain decreases may sleep better, move more, and feel less miserable, and all of those feed into other conditions. So some portion of the reports practitioners collect about effects beyond the spine may be real for reasons that have nothing to do with the theory that generated the prediction. That does not rescue the classical mechanism. It is a different explanation. But a careful person should not dismiss every such report as imaginary, and should ask which explanation is in play.

The fourth gap is massage, and it is quieter. The outcomes most commonly measured are pain, anxiety and sense of well being, measured close to the session, and they are measured by asking people. That is not a criticism, because pain and anxiety are inherently subjective and asking is the only valid instrument we have. But two things stay unclear. One is durability: we know rather less about what a course of massage does over months than about how a session feels at the end of it. The other is mechanism, where the literature is thin and the proposals I listed earlier are at very different levels of support. There is also confounding, which just means a third factor is quietly influencing both the thing you changed and the thing you measured. People who book massages differ from people who do not.

Finally, a note on method rather than content. The historical claims in this chapter are deliberately coarse. Where I have given you a year, as with 1895 in Davenport or the 1987 antitrust finding, that date is checked. Where I have written something vaguer, like the nineteenth century American Midwest, that is me declining to supply a date I have not personally verified, because this book's standard is that a number which sounds authoritative but was recalled rather than checked is the one unforgivable error. I would rather be noticeably vague than quietly wrong.

  • Can you explain how a tradition can be right about what helps while being wrong about why, and then explain why a wrong mechanism is still dangerous rather than merely untidy?
  • Can you define equivocation, and build an argument about subluxation that looks persuasive but proves nothing?

Chapter 4

Mind and Body

When a study says meditation works, works better than what?

In the space of about a week, two people told me about the same study and told me opposite things.

The first was a patient of mine, a woman in her fifties who had been coming in for neck pain and who teaches yoga two evenings a week at a studio near her house. She mentioned, while I was working on her, that there was a big review in a medical journal showing that meditation helps anxiety and depression, and she said it with a certain satisfaction, the way you say something when you have been waiting a while to be able to say it. The second was a physician I trained near, a good clinician with no patience for any of this, who brought up a big review in a medical journal showing that meditation is no better than anything else you might do, and he said that with satisfaction too.

They were talking about the same paper. Neither of them had misread it. Both of their summaries are in it, in the abstract, a few lines apart.

That is the chapter. I will tell you now that I think this is the most important chapter in the book, not because yoga and meditation are the most interesting traditions we will cover, but because this is where the central skill of the course becomes unavoidable. Everywhere else you can get by on general skepticism. Here you cannot, because general skepticism gives you the wrong answer and so does general enthusiasm. The only thing that gets you to the truth is a single question asked at the right moment, and the question is: compared with what?

One more piece of housekeeping before we start, and it is the same housekeeping I promised you in chapter 1. These are not my traditions. I am a chiropractor. I put my hands on people for a living. I have read a great deal about yoga and meditation and I have sat in classes, but I am not a practitioner in any sense a serious practitioner would recognize, and when I describe what happens in a studio or on a cushion I am describing it from the literature and from the outside. Where I think that outsider position makes me miss something, I will say so. You should weight my reading of the research more heavily than my reading of the experience.

What is actually being claimed

Run the first of our three questions. What is the claim, stated the way a believer would state it, at its strongest?

Here is the thing that trips up most students, and most journalists, and quite a few researchers. Neither yoga nor meditation, in the traditions they come from, claims to be a treatment for a symptom. That is not a small technicality. It is the whole shape of the disagreement between the traditions and the clinical literature, and if you miss it you will spend the rest of the chapter answering a question nobody in the tradition asked.

Ask a serious meditation teacher what meditation is for and you will very likely not hear relaxation. You may well hear something closer to the opposite: that the practice involves sitting still with experience you would normally move away from, that it is often uncomfortable, and that the point is a change in how you relate to your own thoughts and sensations rather than a reduction in any particular one of them. The claim is about seeing clearly. Calm, in that account, is a side effect, sometimes, and not the target. Ask a serious yoga teacher what yoga is for and you are unlikely to hear that it is a back exercise. You are more likely to hear that it is a discipline, a comprehensive one, with ethical commitments and breathing practice and attention training in it, and that the postures are one component of a much larger system whose aim is not a more comfortable lumbar spine.

So the strongest version of the claim is something like this. These are trainings, not interventions. They work on the practitioner rather than on a condition. Done seriously over years, they change a person's relationship to pain, fear, craving and thought itself, and the improvements a clinician would notice, less anxiety, better mood, less suffering from the same amount of pain, are downstream of that change rather than the thing being aimed at. A practitioner would add that a training like this cannot be evaluated in eight weeks any more than you could evaluate learning the piano in eight weeks, and that a questionnaire score is a poor instrument for detecting what the practice is actually supposed to do.

That is a real claim and I think it deserves to be stated before it is tested, which is why I have given it a paragraph of its own. Now notice the awkwardness.

The clinical literature does not test that claim. It cannot. What the clinical literature tests is a much smaller and much more manageable claim: that a structured program, usually running about eight weeks, in which people are taught to meditate or to do yoga and asked to practice, produces measurable improvement on standard questionnaires for anxiety, depression or pain, relative to some comparison group. That is the medicalized version. It is narrower than the traditional claim, it uses instruments the traditions did not choose, and it runs on a timescale a practitioner would call absurd.

I want to be fair about who did this. The traditions did not ask to be tested this way. The push to study yoga and meditation as treatments came largely from inside medicine, from clinicians who wanted something to offer patients with chronic pain and anxiety that was not a prescription, and who needed evidence before they could offer it. So the mismatch between what the tradition claims and what the trials measure is not a trick played on the traditions. It is the price of admission to a clinic, and the traditions have mostly been willing to pay it.

But it does mean that when we get to the evidence, we are evaluating the narrow claim. If a trial shows that eight weeks of taught meditation moves an anxiety score a modest amount, that is a fact about eight weeks of taught meditation and an anxiety score. It is not a verdict on whether the practice does what its own tradition says it does over a lifetime. Nobody has tested that, and I am not sure anybody could.

Yoga and meditation as they are actually practiced

Before the mechanisms and before the trials, I want you to have a clear picture of what the actual behavior looks like in the actual population, because the gap between the tradition, the trial and the Tuesday evening class is wide enough to drive a truck through.

Start with how many people we are talking about. Yoga use among United States adults went from 5.0 percent in 2002 to 9.0 percent in 2012 to 15.8 percent in 2022. That is roughly a tripling in twenty years, and it puts yoga in the hands of something like one adult in six. Meditation reached 17.3 percent of adults in 2022, which made it the most used of the seven complementary approaches that survey tracked. More Americans reported meditating than reported any other practice in this book. And the reasons have shifted in a direction worth noticing: among people using yoga, the share who said they were using it for pain management went from 11.4 percent in 2002 to 22.8 percent in 2012 to 28.8 percent in 2022. Yoga is being taken up, increasingly, as something people do about pain.

Now what are those people actually doing?

For most of them, yoga is a class. Sixty minutes, a room with a mirror or no mirror, a teacher at the front, a sequence of postures with some attention to breathing, usually a few minutes of lying still at the end. The pace and the emphasis vary enormously between a gentle class at a community center and a heated class at a boutique studio, and so does the physical demand, which is one of the reasons the research literature is harder to read than it looks. For a growing number of people, yoga is a video on a screen in a living room, with nobody watching to see whether the knee is tracking where it should.

For most people who report meditating, meditation is an application on a phone. Ten minutes of guided audio, sometimes five, a voice telling you where to put your attention, a timer, often a counter telling you how many days in a row you have managed it. Some of those applications are built with real care by people who know the traditions well. The practice they deliver is still a short, guided, solitary, unsupervised thing with a streak counter attached, and that is a different object from a taught program with a teacher who corrects you and a group that notices when you do not show up.

Set that beside the traditions. The traditions as they were transmitted were comprehensive systems. Ethical commitments, diet, breath, study, the conduct of ordinary life, a teacher who knew you, a community, and a stated purpose that was not symptom relief and often was not even health. What a studio class and an application deliver is a slice of that, selected for what can be taught in an hour to a stranger, sold at a price, and fitted into a week that is otherwise unchanged. Two slices, in fact: the physical one in the case of yoga, the attentional one in the case of meditation.

I am not building to an accusation here, and I want to be careful, because this is exactly the kind of observation that gets weaponized. The weaponized version goes: what you are doing is not real yoga, therefore it is worthless. That does not follow at all. A slice of a system can be the useful slice. Willow bark was a comprehensive traditional remedy and aspirin is a slice of it, and the slice turned out to be the part that worked. It is entirely possible that the postures are the part of yoga that helps a sore back, and that stripping away the rest cost you something philosophically while costing you nothing clinically. That is a question for the evidence, not for a sneer.

What the gap does mean is three concrete things for reading this chapter.

First, it means that the word yoga and the word meditation are doing a lot of unearned work. When a trial says meditation, it usually means a specific, structured, taught practice with a manual behind it. When a survey says 17.3 percent of adults meditate, it means whatever those adults said yes to, which includes guided audio, prayer adjacent practices, breathing exercises and sitting quietly. Those are not the same intervention and there is no reason to assume they have the same effects.

Second, it means the trial evidence and the population practice are only loosely connected. Almost all of the serious trial evidence rests on programs taught in person by trained instructors over a fixed number of weeks, with homework and accountability. A review published in 2014 cannot tell you much about an application you downloaded last week, because that application did not exist when those trials were run. When somebody tells you that research supports meditation and then recommends a particular subscription, there is a step missing in the middle, and it is a real step, not a quibble.

Third, it means dose and quality are mostly invisible in the numbers. Two people in the same trial, both assigned to yoga, can have attended nine classes or two, can have practiced at home daily or never, and can have had a teacher who watched their alignment or a teacher who did not look up. The effect size you will meet shortly is an average across all of that.

One last thing about practice as it actually happens, and then we will move on. You will often read that these traditions are thousands of years old. I am not going to assert that in this book, in either direction. The honest situation is that the historical questions, about how much of what is taught in a studio today descends continuously from older systems and how much was assembled, selected and modernized much more recently, are genuinely argued about by people who read the primary sources in the original languages, which I do not. What I will say is that an antiquity claim is doing rhetorical work whenever you hear it. It is offered as evidence. Age is not evidence. Bloodletting had a long and distinguished run. If a practice works, it works for reasons you could in principle demonstrate, and if the only argument offered for it is that it is very old, you have been handed a slogan rather than a reason. That cuts both ways, incidentally. Being recently invented is not evidence against a practice either, and some students overcorrect into thinking it is.

The proposed mechanisms, and how good each one is

Second question. What is the proposed mechanism, the story about why this works?

There are a lot of stories, and the popular literature presents them as a single accumulating pile of support, as though plausible mechanism plus plausible mechanism adds up to a demonstrated one. It does not. So I am going to lay them out and rank them honestly, from the ones I think carry real weight to the ones that are mostly decoration. I am not going to attach numbers to this ranking, because the numbers I would need are not solid enough to print, and I would rather give you my reasoning and let you argue with it than give you false precision.

One. It is exercise. For yoga this is the mechanism I would put first, and it is the one least likely to appear in a studio's marketing. Yoga involves holding your body in demanding positions, loading muscles, moving joints through range, and doing it repeatedly for an hour at a time. We have a large and unglamorous body of knowledge about what happens to deconditioned people with chronic back pain when they start doing structured movement, and it is good. If you wanted to predict in advance whether a yoga program would help a sedentary person's sore back, you would not need any yoga specific theory at all. You would reason from exercise. This mechanism has the strongest support of anything on this list and it is the least exciting, which is a pattern you will see repeatedly in this field.

Two. A changed relationship to thought and sensation. This is the central psychological mechanism proposed for meditation, and it is close to the tradition's own account. The proposal is that practice teaches you to notice a thought or a sensation as an event passing through, rather than as a command or a fact, and that this reduces the amount of suffering you generate on top of whatever is actually happening. In pain specifically, the proposal is that the sensation may be unchanged while the alarm around it drops, and that this is a genuine improvement rather than a trick. I find this mechanism plausible and I think it is coherent with what the trial evidence shows, which is modest improvement in anxiety, depression and pain and much less for things like weight or eating. But I want you to see the weak point: nearly everything we know about it is measured by asking people, which means the mechanism and the outcome are being assessed with the same instrument, a questionnaire filled in by a person who knows what group they are in. That is not nothing. It is also not independent confirmation.

Three. Behavior and structure. A less romantic proposal. Joining a class or committing to a program means putting twenty or sixty minutes in a calendar, leaving the house, being somewhere with other people, and interrupting a day that was otherwise going to be spent sitting. For a depressed or anxious person, that scheduled activity is itself a known lever. This one I would rank as plausible and partly supported, with one honest complication that I think is underappreciated. The large meditation review we are about to read found low evidence of no effect, or not enough evidence to say anything, for sleep, substance use, eating habits and weight. If the mechanism were mostly general life reorganization, you would expect the downstream behaviors to move. They largely did not, at least not in those trials. That is a mark against the broadest version of this story.

Four. Breath and the autonomic nervous system. Here we leave the reasonably supported and enter the plausible. The proposal is that slow controlled breathing shifts the balance of the autonomic nervous system, the part that runs heart rate, digestion and the stress response without your involvement, toward the branch associated with rest, and that this is why people feel calmer. You will see the phrase vagal tone used, referring to the activity of the vagus nerve, the main nerve of that resting branch. Short term physiological changes during slow breathing are real and measurable. The weak link is not there. The weak link is the step from a change in a physiological measure during a practice to a change in how somebody feels in April after practicing since February. That step is asserted far more often than it is shown, and it is the step that matters clinically.

Five. Stress hormones and inflammation. One rung lower. The proposal is that chronic stress raises certain hormones and inflammatory signals, that these contribute to disease, and that these practices lower them. Every link in that chain is individually arguable, the measurements vary between laboratories and across the day, the studies are often small, and the chain has to hold at every link to deliver the conclusion. I would not build a clinical argument on it, and I notice that it is the mechanism most often invoked when the claim being supported is a large one, such as that meditation prevents disease.

Six. Changes in the brain. Bottom of my list, which will surprise some of you because it is top of the list in popular coverage. The proposal is that practice changes the structure or activity of particular brain regions. Brain imaging is genuinely impressive and a picture of a brain is the most persuasive object in modern science journalism. But consider what a difference in an image can and cannot tell you. Anything you do repeatedly for years, including driving a taxi or playing the cello, is apt to show up in some measure of a brain. Showing a difference does not tell you that the difference caused the benefit, does not tell you the benefit occurred, and does not help a patient decide what to do on Monday. If a practice reliably reduced anxiety and we had no brain data at all, I would recommend it. If the brain data were beautiful and the anxiety did not move, I would not. The clinical outcome is the thing. The image is an interesting footnote that has been promoted to a headline.

Seven. The non specific ingredients. I have saved these for last but I would not rank them last for size. Every taught program delivers a bundle: an expectation that you will improve, the attention of a teacher, the company of a group, a ritual, a plausible explanation for your suffering, and the simple fact that you are doing something about a problem instead of enduring it. By non specific I mean ingredients that are not unique to the practice, the ones you would also get from a well run class in anything. These are powerful and they are not fake. A patient who feels better because someone paid attention to her for an hour a week actually feels better.

And this is the hinge of the whole chapter, so slow down here. The entire difficulty of evaluating yoga and meditation is that mechanism seven is always present, always helpful, and almost impossible to remove. The design of a study is nothing more than an attempt to subtract it. How well a study subtracts it determines what the study can prove. That is what the comparison group is for, and that is why the comparison group, which sounds like the most boring part of a trial, is the part that decides what the trial means.

The Goyal review, read properly

Now the paper my patient and my colleague were both quoting.

In 2014, a team led by Goyal published a systematic review and meta analysis of meditation programs in JAMA Internal Medicine. A systematic review means the authors searched the literature in a defined way, decided in advance which studies qualified, and summarized all of them rather than the ones they liked. A meta analysis means they pooled the numbers from those studies into combined estimates. They ended up with 47 randomized trials and 3,515 participants.

Here is the design decision that makes this paper worth your attention. Goyal and colleagues restricted their review to trials that had an active comparison group, specifically in order to deal with placebo effects. They did not pool the easy studies. Think about what that means in light of mechanism seven. If you want to know whether meditation does something beyond the bundle of expectation, attention and doing something, you must compare it against a group that also received a bundle. The authors made that their entry requirement. Many reviews in this field do not.

Now the findings, and I want you to have the actual numbers rather than an adjective, because the adjectives are where this literature goes wrong.

The review found moderate evidence of improvement from mindfulness meditation programs in three areas. Anxiety, with an effect size of 0.38 at 8 weeks, falling to 0.22 at 3 to 6 months. Depression, with an effect size of 0.30 at 8 weeks, falling to 0.23 at 3 to 6 months. And pain, with an effect size of 0.33. For stress, distress and mental health related quality of life, the evidence was low. And for positive mood, attention, substance use, eating habits, sleep and weight, there was either low evidence of no effect or not enough evidence to say anything at all.

That last list deserves a moment, because it is the half of the paper that nobody on either side quotes. Attention is on it. Sleep is on it. If you have been told that meditation sharpens your focus and fixes your sleep, this review, which only looked at trials with a real comparison group, did not find that. I think that is genuinely important and I notice that it appears in almost no popular account.

And then the sentence my colleague was quoting. The authors found no evidence that meditation programs were better than any active treatment, such as drugs, exercise or other behavioral therapies.

Read those two findings together, slowly. Moderate evidence of benefit for anxiety, depression and pain. No evidence of superiority over any active treatment. Both from the same 47 trials. Both true.

How can both be true at once? Because active comparison groups are not all one thing, and this is the distinction that unlocks the paper. Some comparison groups in these trials received something designed to supply the non specific bundle without supplying a treatment known to work: a general health education class, say, with a teacher and a group and homework and an expectation of improvement. Call that a non specific active control. Other comparison groups received a treatment that we already know does something: a medication, a structured exercise program, an established psychological therapy. Call that a specific active treatment. Those are two different benchmarks, and the same intervention can clear one and not the other. Meditation beat the first benchmark modestly. It did not beat the second.

Which is exactly what you would expect if meditation is a real but modest treatment that is about as good as the other real but modest treatments we have for anxiety, depression and pain. Nothing paradoxical is happening. The paper is coherent. It is only confusing if you came to it wanting a verdict.

Now watch the two headlines come out of it.

The enthusiast writes: major JAMA review finds meditation improves anxiety, depression and pain. That headline is accurate. The review did find that, with moderate certainty, against comparison groups that controlled for attention and expectation, which is a harder test than most of this literature sets. My patient was entitled to her satisfaction.

The skeptic writes: major JAMA review finds no evidence meditation beats any active treatment. That headline is also accurate, word for word from the paper. My colleague was entitled to his.

Neither of them is lying. Neither of them is even shading. They have each taken one sentence out of an abstract that contains both, and the sentence each one took is the one that answers the question they already cared about. The enthusiast was answering: is there anything to this? The skeptic was answering: should this replace what I prescribe? Those are different questions and the paper answers both, differently.

There is one more move available here, and it is the move an honest reader has to refuse. You cannot turn no evidence of superiority into evidence of equivalence. It is tempting. It would be very convenient to conclude that meditation is just as good as medication, since nobody showed it was better. That conclusion is not in the paper and it is not supported by the design. Showing that two treatments are genuinely equivalent requires a trial built for that purpose, usually a larger one, with a stated margin for how much difference would count as a real difference. A set of trials that failed to detect a difference may have been too small to detect one, or may have measured the wrong thing, or may have compared the two treatments in patients where neither did much. Not shown to be better is not the same as shown to be the same. Hold the distinction. People will try to take it from you.

What an effect size of 0.3 is, in a life

I have handed you numbers like 0.38 and 0.30 and 0.33 and I have not yet told you what they mean. Most writing on this subject never does, which lets the number function as a vague badge of seriousness. Let us fix that, because once you can feel what 0.3 means you will never be fooled by this literature in either direction.

An effect size is a way of reporting a difference between two groups in units of how spread out the scores were to begin with. The spread is measured by the standard deviation, which is roughly the typical distance between one person's score and the average score in the group. So an effect size of 1.0 means the two group averages sit a full typical distance apart, which is a large gap. An effect size of 0.3 means they sit about a third of a typical distance apart.

Why report it this way at all? Because anxiety questionnaires come in a dozen different scales and a 4 point improvement means nothing until you know whether the scale runs to 20 or to 100. Dividing by the spread strips out the units and lets you pool studies that used different instruments. That is the virtue. The cost is that the number is now abstract, and abstraction is where people hide.

So make it concrete. Suppose we are measuring anxiety on a scale where the people in a trial average 30, and where the typical distance from that average is 10 points, so that a fairly anxious person scores 40 and a fairly calm one scores 20. An effect size of 0.3 in that trial means the meditation group ended up, on average, about 3 points better than the comparison group. Not 3 points better than where they started. Three points better than the people who did the other thing, who also improved, because people in trials generally improve.

Three points out of a 10 point spread. Now ask yourself the honest question. Would you notice 3 points?

Maybe. That is the truthful answer and I am not going to dress it up. Here is another way to picture it. Imagine the two groups as two piles of scores laid side by side. An effect size of 0.3 shifts one pile slightly to the better side of the other. The piles still overlap almost completely. If I handed you one person's score and asked which group they came from, you would have very little idea. Plenty of people in the meditation group did worse than plenty of people in the comparison group. The shift is in the average, and the average is a statement about the group, not a promise to any individual in it.

Which brings up the thing that an average genuinely hides. An average improvement of 3 points is compatible with several very different realities. It is compatible with everybody improving by about 3 points. It is also compatible with a quarter of the group improving substantially, say 12 points, and three quarters of the group getting nothing. The arithmetic is the same. And in general we cannot tell those stories apart from the published summaries, and we cannot predict in advance which group a given person is in. I think the second story is closer to what clinicians see with most of these practices, with some people for whom it is transformative and many for whom it is a pleasant waste of a Tuesday, but I want to flag clearly that this is my impression from practice and not a finding I can cite.

Now, is 0.3 a lot or a little? This is where people get dishonest, usually without meaning to.

The dishonest enthusiast's move is to call 0.3 proof that meditation transforms lives. It is not. Nothing with an effect size of 0.3 transforms lives on average. If a drug company brought me a pill with an effect size of 0.3 on depression and a brochure saying it changes everything, I would raise an eyebrow at the brochure.

The dishonest skeptic's move is to call 0.3 basically nothing, a rounding error, noise. That is wrong in a more interesting way, and I want to spend a paragraph on it because it is the error better educated people make.

A modest effect is worth a great deal when three conditions hold, and in the case of these practices all three tend to hold. The first is that the condition is common. Anxiety, depression and chronic pain are enormously common, and a modest average benefit spread across tens of millions of people is a large amount of human relief, even though no one person experiences it as dramatic. The second is that the treatment is cheap and widely available. A practice you can learn in a community class, or in some cases from an application, and then continue without paying anyone, is a different proposition from a 0.3 effect that requires a specialist, a copay and a drive. The third is that the harms are small. A treatment with a modest benefit and almost no downside can be a perfectly rational thing to try first, and a treatment with the same modest benefit and a serious harm profile is not. We will see in a moment that yoga, in the best review available, carries mostly sore backs and no suggestion of serious harm. That matters to the value of the number.

So the honest summary is the unsatisfying one: modest but real. Those three words are not a fudge and I want you to be comfortable saying them out loud without feeling like you have failed to take a position. Modest but real is a position. It is in fact the single most common correct verdict in this entire field, and students who cannot say it end up forced to choose between two wrong answers.

Two more things to notice in the numbers, both of which teach something.

First, the decay. Anxiety improved by 0.38 at 8 weeks and 0.22 at 3 to 6 months. Depression improved by 0.30 at 8 weeks and 0.23 at 3 to 6 months. The benefit shrinks after the program ends. That is not a scandal and it is not unique to meditation. It is what happens with most behavioral treatments when the teacher goes away, and it tells you something practical: whatever is working here appears to depend on continuing to do it. For a practice that the tradition describes as a lifelong training, that is almost reassuring. It also means an eight week course sold as a permanent fix is overselling.

Second, compare these numbers with the acupuncture pattern elsewhere in this book, where the individual patient data meta analysis found differences of about 0.5 standard deviations against no acupuncture and about 0.2 standard deviations against sham, sham being a convincing imitation of the treatment. Hold those two numbers next to meditation's 0.3 against an active comparison. The lesson is identical and it is the lesson of the whole course. The size of the apparent effect depends enormously on what you compared against. Against nothing, these practices look strong. Against a credible imitation, or against another real treatment, they look modest. Any time somebody quotes you a single effect size with no mention of the comparison group, they have told you almost nothing, and they may not know it.

Waitlists, active controls and what each can prove

Time to make the machinery explicit. I want you to be able to look at any trial in this field, find one sentence in the methods, and know what the study is capable of proving before you read a single result.

The sentence is the one describing the comparison group. Here is the ladder, from weakest to strongest.

No treatment, or usual care. The comparison group simply carries on. Whatever happens to them is the baseline. This design can show that a treated group ended up better than an untreated group, which is worth knowing, but it cannot separate the treatment from anything else that comes with being treated.

A waitlist. The most common design in this literature and worth understanding properly. Participants are randomly assigned either to start the eight week yoga or meditation program now, or to be told they will receive it in eight weeks. Everyone eventually gets the program, which makes the design easy to recruit for and easy to defend ethically, and it is cheap. You do not have to invent and staff a credible alternative program.

Now look at everything a waitlist fails to subtract. The treated group has an expectation of improvement and the waitlist group has an expectation of nothing happening yet. The treated group gets a teacher's attention, a room full of people, a plausible story about their problem and a sense of acting on it. The waitlist group gets a letter. Both groups fill in the same questionnaire at the end, and the treated group knows perfectly well that they are the ones who did the thing, which affects how people answer questions about how they feel. On top of all that, people usually enroll in a trial when their symptoms are bad, and bad symptoms often ease on their own, so both groups drift toward the middle for reasons having nothing to do with treatment. The design does not remove that either.

And there is a sharper problem that students find surprising. A waitlist is not neutral. Being told you must wait eight weeks for the help you signed up for can be mildly demoralizing, and it can also cause people to put off other things they might have done in the meantime, since help is coming. If a waitlist does slightly worse than genuinely doing nothing, then the gap between treatment and waitlist overstates the treatment's benefit, because part of the gap is the waitlist sinking rather than the treatment rising. We cannot usually tell how much of that is happening. It is not a reason to throw out waitlist trials. It is a reason to read their effect sizes as an upper bound.

A non specific active control. The comparison group gets a real program with real ingredients, just not the ingredients under test. A general health education class with a teacher, a group, handouts and homework. A relaxation group. Something that delivers attention, expectation, time and ritual. If the treated group still comes out ahead of that, you have learned something considerably more interesting, because you have subtracted most of mechanism seven. This is the benchmark that produced meditation's 0.38 and 0.30 and 0.33, which is exactly why those numbers are smaller than the ones you will see quoted from waitlist trials, and exactly why they are worth more.

An active treatment known to work. The comparison group gets a medication, an established psychological therapy, or a structured exercise program. Now the question is no longer whether the practice does anything. It is whether the practice does more than the thing we already have. This is the benchmark the Goyal review reported no evidence of meditation beating. It is a hard benchmark, it is the one that matters for a clinician choosing between options, and it is the one that almost never makes a headline.

A sham. The comparison group gets something designed to be indistinguishable from the real treatment while lacking its supposedly active component. A needle that does not penetrate. A pill with no drug in it. If the real thing beats a convincing imitation of itself, you have evidence that the specific component matters and not merely the ritual around it.

Here is where yoga and meditation are in a genuinely difficult position, and I want to be straightforward that this is a limitation of the subject rather than a failing of the researchers. You cannot sham these practices. The Cochrane review of yoga for back pain notes explicitly that no trial compared yoga with sham yoga, and that participants knew which group they were in. Think about what a sham yoga class would even be. A class of movements carefully designed to look like yoga while not being yoga, taught by somebody who must not let on, to students who have very likely seen yoga before. And meditation is worse. To blind someone to whether they are meditating you would have to teach them a practice that is not meditation while they believe it is, which is close to a contradiction, since the practice is what is going on in their own attention.

So the top rung of the ladder is not available here, and it never will be. That is a permanent and irreducible gap in our knowledge about these practices, and nobody should pretend otherwise. The best available design is the non specific active control, which is why I want you to hold the Goyal review in high regard even though its findings are modest. It climbed as high as the subject allows.

Let me put a person in this so it stops being abstract. Marcus is 46, drives for a living, and has had a low grade ache across his lower back for two years that flares up badly every few months. He has tried ibuprofen and a few sessions of physical therapy he stopped attending. His sister tells him yoga fixed her back and sends him an article headlined that yoga reduces chronic back pain.

Before this chapter, Marcus has two options: believe his sister or dismiss her. After this chapter, he has a question. What did the article's study compare yoga against? If the answer is a waitlist, then the study tells Marcus that doing a yoga program beats being told to wait, which is useful and is not the same as knowing yoga is the right choice for him. If the answer is another form of back exercise, then the study is answering the question Marcus actually has, because Marcus is not choosing between yoga and nothing. He is choosing between yoga, going back to the physical therapist, swimming, or continuing as he is. That is a comparison of active options, and only a trial with an active comparison speaks to it.

That shift, from believing or dismissing to asking what the comparison was, is the entire skill. It is portable. It works on a supplement advertisement, a new drug, a teaching method and a diet. And it costs nothing to apply, which is why I will keep insisting on it until the end of the course.

The Cochrane yoga review, same lesson from the other side

Now the second anchor, and it makes the same point coming from the opposite direction, which is why I have put the two chapters' worth of argument in one chapter.

In 2022, Wieland and colleagues published a Cochrane review of yoga for chronic non specific low back pain. Two pieces of vocabulary first. Cochrane is an international organization that produces systematic reviews to an unusually strict and standardized method, and its reviews are generally the most conservative summary available on a question, which means that when a Cochrane review says probably, it has earned the word. Non specific low back pain means back pain with no identified structural cause: no fracture, no tumor, no nerve compression explaining it. That description covers the large majority of back pain, including, almost certainly, Marcus.

The review's two central findings, in order.

First, yoga is probably better than no exercise for pain and for back related function at three months, with small improvements. That is the sentence a yoga studio would put on its website, and it is fair. Notice its two qualifications though, because they are both doing work. Better than no exercise, which is the lower rung of our ladder. And small improvements, which is the reviewers' own characterization, not a skeptic's spin.

Second, compared with other back focused exercise, there is probably little or no difference in back related function, and the evidence on pain for that comparison is uncertain. There it is. Climb one rung up the ladder, from no exercise to an active treatment we already had, and the advantage of yoga specifically mostly disappears for function, and we cannot say with any confidence what happens to pain.

Put the two anchors side by side now and notice that they are the same result in different clothing. Meditation: moderate evidence of benefit, no evidence of superiority over active treatment. Yoga: probably better than no exercise, probably no different from other back focused exercise. In both cases the practice does something. In both cases it has not been shown to beat the alternative that was already available. If you remember one shape from this chapter, remember that one, because it recurs throughout this book and you will be able to predict it before you read the study.

The Cochrane reviewers also tell you how much confidence to place in their own conclusions, which is a practice I wish were universal. They downgraded the certainty of the evidence to moderate, low or very low depending on the comparison, and they said why. No trial compared yoga with sham yoga. Participants knew which group they were in. Some of the trials were very small. And some comparisons rested on few trials, or on trials that disagreed with each other.

Sit with that list, because it is a short course in how evidence gets graded. Every item on it is a reason the true effect might be different from the measured one, and none of them is an accusation of bad faith. The researchers did not fail. Some of these problems cannot be solved, as we established with sham yoga. What the reviewers have done is tell you exactly how much weight the floor will bear. When somebody cites this review as proving that yoga works for back pain, they are standing on a floor the builders labeled moderate to very low, and they usually have not read the label.

Then the harms, which I want to handle carefully because this is where a book like this can do real damage in either direction.

The review found that back pain was the most commonly reported harm. It occurred more often with yoga than with no yoga, and at a similar rate to other exercise. There was no suggestion of serious harm.

Read that the way you have now learned to read everything else. Yes, yoga caused more back pain than not doing yoga. Of course it did. Asking a deconditioned person with a sore back to hold demanding positions for an hour will sometimes make the back sore. But the comparison that matters is not yoga against nothing, because nothing is not really on Marcus's menu and doing nothing about chronic back pain has its own costs. The comparison that matters is yoga against the other active thing he might do, and against other exercise the harm rate was similar. So the honest statement to a patient is this: this will sometimes make your back ache, about as often as any other exercise program for your back would, and nothing in the best available review suggests you are risking anything serious.

That is a sentence I would be comfortable saying in clinic. Notice that it neither scares Marcus away from something safe nor reassures him about a risk that exists. Both of those failures are real harms to a patient, and the second one is the failure this field is better known for, but I have seen the first one plenty of times too, usually from clinicians who had decided in advance what they thought.

One more honest observation about the yoga evidence while we are here. The Cochrane finding that yoga is roughly equivalent to other back focused exercise is often reported as bad news for yoga. I do not think it is, and here is why. If two treatments work about equally well, the choice between them should be made on everything else: cost, access, whether a person will actually keep doing it, whether they enjoy it, whether there is a class near their house at a time they can attend. Adherence is not a footnote in chronic pain. It is most of the game. A person who will go to a yoga class twice a week for a year and would not have done the home exercise sheet twice in a month is better off with yoga, and no trial captures that, because trials measure what happens when people are enrolled and supervised. The evidence says choose on other grounds. It does not say do not choose this.

The mirror image of that reasoning also has to be said. If the practice is being used instead of care that would have worked, the modest effect size becomes a serious problem. Equivalence to exercise for non specific back pain is one thing. The Goyal review covered anxiety, depression and pain, and it did not test meditation as a replacement for treatment of severe psychiatric illness. Nothing in this chapter supports substituting a practice with a 0.3 effect on depression for treatment of someone who is severely ill, and remember that not shown to be worse is not the same as shown to be equivalent. This is the complementary and alternative distinction from chapter 1 arriving exactly where I told you it would arrive. The same practice, with the same evidence behind it, is a reasonable addition in one patient and a dangerous substitution in another, and nothing about the yoga or the meditation has changed between them.

Where the evidence runs out

Here is what I do not know, and what nobody does.

I do not know whether these practices do what their own traditions claim, because that has not been studied and I am not sure how it could be. Everything in this chapter concerns eight week programs measured with questionnaires. The traditional claim is about what happens to a person over decades of serious practice with a teacher, and about qualities that no instrument I know of measures. The honest position is not that the traditional claim is false. It is that it is outside the reach of the evidence we have, and the evidence we have was built to answer a clinician's question instead.

I cannot tell you what happens past six months. The meditation numbers stop at 3 to 6 months and the yoga findings are at three months. Both of these are practices people take up for years. The research covers the first few weeks of a long relationship and then goes quiet.

I cannot separate the specific from the non specific as cleanly as I would like, and no design that exists can. There is no sham yoga and there is no sham meditation. Participants always know what they were assigned to. That gap is permanent, and anybody who tells you that these practices have been shown to work through a specific mechanism, as opposed to working, is going beyond what the designs can support.

I cannot tell you who will benefit. The averages are modest, the variation underneath them is probably large, and we have no reliable way to look at a particular patient and say that this one is likely to be a responder. In practice this means a trial of the thing itself, undertaken with some honesty about whether it is helping, is often the only way to find out.

I cannot tell you that the application on your phone does what the taught programs in those trials did. It might. It is cheaper, more available and more private than a class, all of which matter. But the trials studied structured taught programs, mostly before the current generation of applications existed, and transferring their results onto a subscription product is an assumption, not a finding. I would like somebody to run that study properly.

And I cannot tell you how much of the yoga literature applies to the yoga your local studio teaches. Yoga in a trial is a defined program. Yoga in a city is a hundred different classes of wildly different intensity, taught by teachers of wildly different training, to people of wildly different fitness. The word is the same. The intervention is not.

None of these gaps is a reason to dismiss yoga or meditation, and none of them is a reason to oversell them. They are the shape of what we know. A good practitioner works inside that shape instead of pretending it is filled in.

Before you move on

  • Can you state the strongest version of what yoga and meditation claim to do, in the traditions' own terms, and then explain how the claim that gets tested in trials is narrower?
  • Can you quote both halves of the Goyal review, the moderate evidence of benefit and the absence of superiority over active treatment, and explain how both can be true of the same 47 trials?
  • Can you explain, to someone with no statistics, what an effect size of 0.3 means in a life, and defend the verdict modest but real against both an enthusiast and a skeptic?
  • Can you list the rungs of the comparison ladder, from no treatment up to sham, and say what question each one answers?
  • Can you say why no sham yoga trial exists, why that gap is permanent, and what it costs us to know?
  • Can you rank the proposed mechanisms for these practices by how well supported each is, and say which one popular coverage overrates most?

Chapter 5

Whole Systems

How do you evaluate a tradition that comes with its own physiology?

Everything we have looked at so far has been a treatment. A spinal adjustment. A sequence of postures. Twenty minutes of attention to the breath. Those are things a person does, or has done to them, and you can ask the three questions of each one without too much difficulty. What is the claim. What is the proposed mechanism. What does the evidence show, and compared with what.

This week is different, and I want to tell you why before we start, because if you treat this chapter like the last two you will get confused and conclude that I am being evasive.

Ayurveda and Traditional Chinese medicine are not treatments. They are complete medical systems. Each one has its own account of what a body is made of and how it works. Each one has its own vocabulary for what has gone wrong, and that vocabulary does not map cleanly onto the one you learned in your anatomy course. Each one has its own set of diagnostic procedures, its own logic for choosing a treatment, its own criteria for improvement, and its own training pathway. A practitioner in either tradition is not offering you a technique. He is offering you an entire alternative description of your body, along with a plan that follows from that description.

That changes the evaluation problem completely, and it changes it in a way that most popular writing on this subject gets wrong in both directions. The enthusiast says you cannot judge a whole system by the standards of a drug trial, which is partly true and is also the sort of thing people say when they do not want to be judged. The skeptic says the underlying physiology is wrong, so there is nothing further to discuss, which skips the inconvenient fact that one of the cleanest results in this entire book comes out of one of these systems. Neither of those positions is going to be good enough for you by the end of the week.

Where I am standing

A word about me, in keeping with what I promised you in chapter 1.

I have studied both of these traditions. I have read the translated classical literature, the modern textbooks that practitioners actually train from, and a great deal of the clinical research. I have referred patients to practitioners of both. I have sat in on treatments and asked a lot of questions, some of them probably annoying.

I do not practice either one. I am not a vaidya and I am not a licensed acupuncturist. When I write about chiropractic, which I did two chapters ago, I am writing partly from inside, with the bias that comes with that, which is why I told you to read that chapter harder. When I write about ayurveda and Traditional Chinese medicine, I am writing from the literature and from observation, and I want that on the record. If your instructor in a later course trained in one of these systems, she will know things about the texture of the work that I do not, and you should listen to her on that. What I can offer you is a careful reading of what each system says about itself and a careful reading of what has been tested. That is not the same as practitioner knowledge, and I am not going to pretend it is.

One more thing before we begin. I am going to spend a long time on each system, describing it in its own words without evaluating it, and that is deliberate. You cannot weigh an idea you cannot state. A student who can only describe ayurveda as the thing with the three body types has not earned an opinion about it. Evaluation comes second. It always comes second.

Ayurveda on its own terms

Ayurveda is the classical medical tradition of the Indian subcontinent. The word is usually rendered as knowledge of life or science of longevity. It survives today as a living professional practice, taught in universities in India, regulated by government bodies there, and practiced around the world, including in the United States, where its legal status varies enormously from state to state and where it is often delivered as consultation and advice rather than as licensed medical care.

Its physiology begins with the idea that everything in the world, including a body, is composed of five fundamental elements: space, air, fire, water and earth. These are not meant as a chemistry. They are categories of property and behavior. Fire is the principle of transformation wherever transformation occurs. Earth is the principle of solidity and mass. If that sounds unlike anything in your biology textbook, good, because it is unlike anything in your biology textbook, and the first mistake students make here is to try to translate.

From those five elements come the three doshas, which are the central working concept of the entire system. A dosha is a functional principle, a bundle of tendencies that governs a set of processes in the body. There are three.

Vata arises from space and air. It is the principle of movement: breath, circulation, nerve impulse, the passage of food through the gut, the movement of thought. Its qualities are described as dry, light, cold, mobile and irregular.

Pitta arises from fire and water. It is the principle of transformation: digestion, metabolism, the conversion of one thing into another, and by extension comprehension and the processing of experience. Its qualities are hot, sharp, light, oily and spreading.

Kapha arises from water and earth. It is the principle of structure, cohesion and lubrication: the tissues that hold shape, the fluids that cushion joints, the stability that keeps a system from flying apart. Its qualities are heavy, cold, slow, stable and oily.

Every person, in this account, is born with a particular proportion of the three. That native proportion is called prakriti, which simply means your constitution, the baseline mix you came with and will have for life. Most people are described as having one or two doshas that predominate. Your prakriti is not a disease and it is not a problem. It is who you physiologically are, and the system treats it as the reference point against which everything else is measured.

What can change is your current state, called vikriti, meaning your present condition and specifically the ways it has departed from your baseline. Illness, in ayurveda, is a departure of the current state from the constitutional one. A person with a naturally high vata constitution who is dry, restless, cold and sleeping badly is not necessarily ill. A person with a naturally high kapha constitution who presents the same way has moved a long distance from where she belongs, and that movement is the thing to be treated. The same symptom means different things in different people, and the treatment differs accordingly.

Notice how that works as clinical reasoning, independent of whether the underlying physiology is correct. It is an explicitly individualizing framework, which builds in at the level of theory the observation that two patients with identical complaints may need different things.

Two more concepts you need, because they do most of the day to day work in the clinic.

Agni means fire, and in this context it means digestive and metabolic capacity: the power to break down, absorb and assimilate. Strong agni is close to the center of ayurvedic ideas about health. Food that is appropriate for you, taken at the right time, in the right quantity, when your capacity to process it is strong, builds good tissue. The same food taken when capacity is weak does not.

What happens when it is not processed is called ama, a residue of incomplete digestion that is held to accumulate, obstruct the body's channels and cause disease at a distance from where it formed. Ama is a crucial idea because it supplies the tradition's answer to a question every medical system has to answer: how does something that happens in the gut make your joints hurt. Conventional medicine has its own answers to that family of questions, some of them good. Ayurveda got there by a different route and much earlier.

Around these sit the dhatus, the seven bodily tissues, formed in sequence from the products of digestion, and the srotas, the channels through which substances move. Obstruction of a channel is a recurring explanatory move. And there is ojas, usually translated as vitality or essence, the refined product of good digestion and good living, which confers resilience.

Diagnosis uses the practitioner's senses and a long interview. The practitioner looks: build, skin, hair, nails, eyes, tongue. He asks, at length, about appetite, digestion, elimination, sleep, energy across the day, temperature preference, response to seasons, emotional tendencies, and the history of all of it. And he palpates the pulse. Pulse examination in ayurveda, called nadi pariksha, is not pulse rate. It is a trained reading of qualities in the pulse at several positions, interpreted as information about the doshas. That is a claim about what a finger can detect, and it is the sort of claim that can in principle be tested, which is a point I will come back to.

Treatment follows from the diagnosis along two broad lines. The first is palliation, which means reducing an excess by opposing it with its opposite qualities. If vata is dry, light, cold and mobile, you counter it with warmth, oil, heaviness, regularity and rest. If pitta is hot and sharp, you cool. Much of this is delivered through food, and here you meet the six tastes, sweet, sour, salty, pungent, bitter and astringent, each of which is held to act on the doshas in a specific direction. Diet in ayurveda is not an adjunct to treatment. It is a primary therapeutic tool, and so is routine. The tradition has a developed literature on daily routine and on seasonal routine, on the grounds that the body's processes have rhythms and that living against them produces illness.

The second line is purification, the removal of accumulated ama and excess dosha rather than merely balancing it. This is the domain of panchakarma, the five actions, a set of cleansing procedures preceded by preparatory oleation and sweating therapies to mobilize what is to be removed. Panchakarma is intensive, is traditionally done under supervision over a period of days or weeks, and is the part of the tradition most likely to be sold to Westerners in a softened spa version that has very little to do with the classical procedure.

Alongside both lines sit the herbal preparations, which is what most Americans encounter first and often the only part they encounter. The classical pharmacology here is large and it is formulary rather than single agent: preparations typically combine many plant materials, and some categories of preparation involve processed minerals and metals. Hold on to that last clause. We are coming back to it in the safety section and it is going to matter.

Finally, ayurveda does not present itself as a treatment system alone. It presents itself as a complete regimen for living, with sections on hygiene, conduct, sexual activity, exercise, sleep, the ethics of the practitioner and the preparation of food. Yoga and ayurveda grew up in the same intellectual world and share a good deal of vocabulary, which is why the two are so often offered together, though they are not the same thing and a yoga teacher is not thereby an ayurvedic practitioner.

Stated that way, at its strongest, ayurveda is an individualized, constitution based, lifestyle centered system that treats digestion as central, understands disease as a departure from a person's own baseline rather than from a population norm, and intervenes mainly through food, routine, herbs and periodic purification. Whatever you end up concluding about it, that is the thing you are concluding about. Not a horoscope. Not a body type quiz.

Traditional Chinese medicine on its own terms

Traditional Chinese medicine, usually abbreviated TCM, is the other great system you will meet in this course, and it is the one with the research result I keep promising you.

Its physiology begins with qi, a word with no clean English equivalent, usually rendered as vital energy and better understood as functional activity: the capacity of a thing to do what it does. Qi is not a substance you could isolate in a tube, and practitioners generally do not claim it is. It circulates, it can be abundant or deficient, it can move freely or become stuck, and most of the clinical reasoning in the system is about those states. Alongside qi the tradition recognizes blood, which overlaps with but is not identical to what a hematologist means, essence, which is a kind of constitutional reserve, spirit, which covers consciousness and mental life, and body fluids.

Organizing all of it is the pairing of yin and yang, which is the single most misunderstood idea in this book. Yin and yang are not good and evil. They are not even fixed categories. They are a relational vocabulary for describing complementary opposites: cool and warm, still and active, substantial and functional, interior and exterior. Nothing is simply yin. A thing is yin relative to something else. The clinical use of this is to describe a state as too hot or too cold, too depleted or too active, and the aim is not to maximize either side but to restore the relationship between them.

Layered on top is the five phase theory, which sorts phenomena into wood, fire, earth, metal and water, and specifies relationships among them: each phase generates the next and each restrains another. This gives the system a way to reason about how a problem in one domain produces a problem in another, which is the same explanatory job that ama does in ayurveda and that systemic inflammation does in a modern pathophysiology lecture.

Then come the organs, and here is where translation causes the most trouble. TCM names a set of organ systems, the zang fu, using words that were translated into English as liver, spleen, kidney, heart, lung and so on. Those translations have done enormous damage to Western understanding of the system, because the TCM liver is not the organ your anatomy instructor dissected. It is a functional system credited with storing blood and with ensuring the smooth, unobstructed movement of qi throughout the body, and it is implicated in states a Western clinician would file under irritability, menstrual complaints and tension. The TCM spleen governs the transformation of food into usable substance and the transport of it, which is closer to a digestive and metabolic function than to the immune organ of the same English name. When a practitioner says spleen qi deficiency, he is not making a claim about your spleen. He is naming a pattern.

Which brings us to the heart of the system, and to the thing that makes it genuinely hard to study. In TCM the object of diagnosis is not a disease. It is a pattern. Pattern differentiation means assembling everything observed about a patient into a named configuration: liver qi stagnation, spleen qi deficiency, damp heat in the lower burner, blood stasis, kidney yang deficiency. Patterns are built using a small set of organizing axes, whether the problem is interior or exterior, hot or cold, deficient or excessive, yin or yang in character, and then specified by the organ systems and substances involved.

The consequence is a slogan practitioners actually use: same disease, different treatment; different diseases, same treatment. Three patients who all arrive with a Western diagnosis of migraine may be assigned three different patterns and receive three different treatments. A patient with insomnia and a patient with eczema may be assigned the same pattern and receive the same treatment. Read that twice, because it is the single biggest obstacle to running a conventional trial of this system, and we will be using it later.

Diagnosis proceeds through four examinations. Looking, which includes a detailed inspection of the tongue, its color, coating, shape and moisture, treated as a readable surface. Listening and smelling, which includes the quality of the voice and breath. Asking, which is a long structured interview covering temperature, sweating, appetite, taste in the mouth, digestion, urine and stool, sleep, pain, emotional state and, for women, the menstrual history in detail. And palpating, which above all means the pulse: taken at three positions on each wrist, at more than one depth, and read for a long list of named qualities such as wiry, slippery, thin, rapid or deep. As with the ayurvedic pulse, this is a specific empirical claim about what a trained finger can detect and distinguish.

Treatment is a toolkit, and acupuncture is only one tool in it. The others matter and are routinely ignored in Western discussion.

Acupuncture is the insertion of fine needles at specified points. The classical account places these points along channels, often translated as meridians, through which qi is held to circulate, and selects them to move what is stuck, supplement what is deficient, or drain what is excessive.

Moxibustion is the application of heat by burning prepared mugwort on or near the skin, used where the pattern calls for warming.

Cupping applies suction to the skin, used for stagnation and for musculoskeletal complaints. It is the one that leaves the circular marks you may have seen on an athlete.

Tui na is a manual therapy, a system of pressing, kneading and manipulating.

Herbal medicine is, in volume of practice, arguably the core of the tradition rather than acupuncture. It works through formulas rather than single herbs: a classical formula with a known composition and purpose is selected to match the pattern and then modified for the individual patient, adding and subtracting ingredients and adjusting quantities. A practitioner trained in China is typically trained in both herbs and needles, and may regard the herbs as the more powerful instrument. In the United States, the public image of TCM is almost entirely acupuncture, which is a distortion worth knowing about.

And alongside these sit dietary therapy and the movement practices, qigong and taiji, which belong to the same intellectual world.

Stated at its strongest, TCM is a pattern based, highly individualized system with a sophisticated diagnostic procedure, a large materia medica organized into modifiable formulas, several physical modalities, and an explicit theory of how disturbance in one functional domain propagates into another. It has been developed, debated and revised by literate practitioners over a very long period, with a substantial written clinical literature. That is what is on the table.

A question about age, raised honestly

You will read, constantly, that these systems are thousands of years old. The claim is usually offered as evidence, on the reasoning that something which has survived that long must be doing something. I want to raise a question about it and then leave it with you, because it is more interesting as a question than as a verdict.

The question is: what exactly is being claimed to be that old? Both of these traditions have long textual histories, and nobody serious disputes that. But the practice that a licensed acupuncturist delivers in Portland this afternoon, and the practice an ayurvedic physician delivers in a hospital in Kerala, are the products of institutional histories much closer to the present: standardized curricula, textbooks written for colleges, national licensing examinations, consolidation of competing regional schools into a teachable synthesis, and in both cases substantial reorganization during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries as these traditions came into contact and competition with Western medicine. The modern systems have real roots in the classical literature. They are not identical to it, and the differences are not small.

I am not saying this to score a point. I am saying it because the antiquity claim is doing argumentative work that it cannot support, and because the reverse argument is just as bad. Age is not evidence of correctness. Bloodletting was practiced in the West for a very long time by intelligent people. Age is also not evidence of incorrectness. Willow bark worked before anyone could tell you why, and it still works, and we call it aspirin now. When somebody tells you a treatment is ancient, the only honest response is that this is a fact about history and not a fact about efficacy, and then to go find out about the efficacy.

Why whole systems are harder to study

Now the methodological problem, which is the real content of this chapter and which I promise is more interesting than it sounds.

When you test a drug, you can hold the intervention still. Every patient in the treatment arm gets the same molecule at the same dose. That is what makes the comparison meaningful: the only thing that differs between the two groups is the thing you are testing.

You cannot do that with a whole system, because in a whole system the individualization is not a nuisance. It is the claimed active ingredient. The TCM practitioner says that three migraine patients need three different treatments. The ayurvedic practitioner says that the right diet for a high pitta patient is wrong for a high vata patient. If you force every patient in your treatment arm to receive the same needles at the same points, or the same herbal formula, you have produced a tidy trial of something the system does not actually claim to do.

So researchers have two broad choices, and each one buys something by giving something up.

The first choice is to test a component. Take acupuncture out of the system, standardize it, and compare it with something. This is what almost all of the acupuncture literature does, and it has a huge advantage: you can construct a comparison that looks and feels like the real thing to the patient, which lets you ask whether the specific part of the treatment is doing anything. The cost is that you have amputated the individualization. A standardized needling protocol for knee pain is not what a TCM practitioner would deliver, and practitioners say so, with some justice.

The second choice is to test the package. Send patients to real practitioners, let them do what they normally do, however they normally do it, and compare the outcomes with usual care or with a waiting list. This is sometimes called whole system or pragmatic research, and it answers a question patients actually care about: if I go see this person, am I likely to end up better off than if I do not? The cost is that you can no longer blind anybody, and you can no longer separate the system from everything that travels with it. The patient knows she is being treated. She has been listened to for an hour, touched, taken seriously, given a coherent explanation of her suffering, and handed a plan. Those are powerful things. They are also not what the theory under test claims to be doing.

There is a third difficulty, subtler than either, and it is about outcome measures. The systems do not primarily aim at the things trials measure. Ayurveda aims at balance relative to constitution, strong agni, good ojas. TCM aims at a resolved pattern. A trial measures pain on a numerical scale, or a validated function questionnaire, or a lab value. You can insist that the trial outcome is the one that matters, and for many purposes I think you should, because a patient who still hurts has not been helped by a resolved pattern. But notice that you have substituted your outcome for theirs, and that if the system is doing something your instrument does not capture, your instrument will not capture it.

And there is a fourth, which is about diagnosis itself, and which I regard as the most important unanswered question in this whole area. Both systems rest on diagnostic procedures that make empirical claims. The pulse readings claim that trained fingers can detect and name specific qualities. Tongue inspection claims that specific features correspond to specific internal states. Pattern differentiation claims that a trained practitioner, given a patient, will arrive at the correct pattern.

Every one of those is testable. You can send the same patient to several practitioners and see whether they agree. You can see whether the agreement is better than chance, and whether it is better than what you would get from two physicians reading the same chest film, which is not perfect either. That is a fair and answerable question, and it would tell us something important about the foundation of both systems.

I am not going to give you a number for it. The honest reason is that this book has a fact base, every number in it has been checked against a source that someone read, and there is no checked entry on diagnostic agreement in either tradition. I would rather hand you a labeled hole than a figure I half remember. Mark it down as something you could go find out, and be careful of anyone who tells you confidently in either direction.

Acupuncture: the cleanest result in the book

Now the good part. For all the difficulty I have just described, one component of one of these systems has been studied better than almost anything else in this course, and the result is unusually clear. I want to take it slowly, because the way to misread it is to stop reading halfway.

The study is an individual patient data meta analysis of acupuncture for chronic pain. Two pieces of jargon there, so let me unpack them.

A meta analysis combines the results of many separate trials to get a more reliable answer than any one of them could give. An ordinary meta analysis works from the summary numbers that each trial published. An individual patient data meta analysis goes much further: the researchers obtain the original records for each individual patient from the investigators who ran each trial, and reanalyze all of it together from scratch. That is harder, slower and far better. It means the analysts are not stuck with whatever each original team chose to report, they can check and standardize, and they can look at questions the original trials did not ask. When you see individual patient data on a meta analysis, pay attention. It is the strongest form of this kind of evidence.

This one pooled 39 trials and 20,827 patients, having added 13 trials to an earlier version of the same analysis. That is a very large body of evidence by the standards of anything in this book.

Here is what it found. Acupuncture was superior to both of its comparison conditions, for every pain condition the review examined, with a probability of less than one in a thousand that the differences arose by chance. That is what P less than .001 means: it is a statement about how unlikely this pattern of results would be if acupuncture were doing nothing at all. It is not a statement about how large the benefit is. Size is a separate question and it is the whole story here.

The analysis had two comparison groups, and this is the part I want you to be able to recite.

Against no acupuncture, meaning patients who were put on a waiting list or continued with their usual care, acupuncture patients did better by about 0.5 standard deviations.

Against sham, meaning patients who received a procedure designed to resemble acupuncture while leaving out what the theory says is the active part, acupuncture patients did better by about 0.2 standard deviations.

Two terms to define, and then we can think.

A sham treatment is a fake that is meant to feel real. In a drug trial the fake is easy: an identical looking pill with nothing in it. In acupuncture it is hard, and researchers have used several approaches: needles that retract into their handles instead of penetrating, real needles inserted shallowly, or real needles inserted at locations the classical map says are not therapeutic points. Each of those leaves out something the theory says matters while keeping the setting, the practitioner's attention, the sensation of something being done to the skin and the patient's expectation of benefit.

A standard deviation is a measure of how spread out a set of numbers is. Saying a treatment helped by 0.5 standard deviations means the average treated patient ended up half of that typical spread better off than the average untreated patient. The reason researchers use this unit is that it lets you combine trials that measured pain on different scales. As a rough translation, and these conventions are rough, a difference of about 0.2 standard deviations is usually called small, 0.5 moderate, 0.8 large. So this study found a moderate benefit against no treatment and a small benefit against a convincing fake.

Two more findings, both important.

The benefit lasted. At one year, the effect had declined by about 15 percent. Not vanished. Declined modestly, and was still there. That is not what you would predict for something driven entirely by the novelty of a new treatment and the enthusiasm of a first visit.

And the size of the measured effect varied mainly with what the control group received, not with the acupuncture technique used. Sit with that one. Across 39 trials, the thing that best predicted how impressive acupuncture looked was not how it was done. It was what it was compared against.

The authors' own conclusion was that acupuncture is effective for chronic pain, and that the reductions in pain cannot be explained solely by placebo effects.

Specific and non specific effects, and why the gap matters

Now we come to the most useful single piece of reasoning in this course, and I want to teach it properly, because if you understand the distance between 0.5 and 0.2 you will be able to read half the literature in this field without being pushed around by it.

Those two numbers are not two estimates of the same thing. They are measurements of two different things, and both measurements are good.

The 0.5 is the total effect of receiving acupuncture. It is everything that comes with the treatment, bundled: the needles, and also the long intake interview, the practitioner's attention, the physical contact, the quiet room, the explanation of your symptoms in a framework that makes them cohere, your own expectation of relief, the ritual of going somewhere and being treated, the natural tendency of a fluctuating condition to improve from the moment you finally sought help, and whatever the needles themselves are doing. That bundle is what a patient actually receives. It produced a moderate improvement.

The 0.2 is the specific effect attributable to the part the theory says is active: real needles, inserted properly, at the places the tradition specifies. It is what you get when you subtract the bundle from the bundle plus the needles. It is small.

And therefore: most of what a patient experiences from acupuncture is real, and most of it is not specific to needle placement. Both halves of that sentence come out of the same study. Neither half is a concession and neither half is a gotcha. If you can hold both without flinching, you are doing this course correctly.

What makes this genuinely interesting, rather than merely deflationary, is that a thoughtful clinician and a thoughtful theorist can read these identical numbers and reach opposite conclusions. Both of them have a case. I am going to make each case at its strongest, because that is the only honest way to do this, and then tell you what I think.

The clinician's reading. Her patient is a fifty six year old woman named Marisol with eight years of knee pain who has tried the drugs she can tolerate and does not want surgery yet. Marisol does not care about needle specificity. She cares whether she can get through a shift. The number that describes what Marisol will experience is 0.5, not 0.2, because 0.5 is what receiving acupuncture produced and receiving acupuncture is the thing on offer. There is no version of this where you hand her the 0.2 by itself.

And, the clinician continues, the 0.2 is not nothing. It was highly statistically reliable across 20,827 patients. Keep in mind what it is being compared against: sham acupuncture is not an inert sugar pill. It involves a practitioner, a room, contact with the skin, often actual penetration of the skin, and a full dose of expectation. You are not comparing acupuncture with nothing. You are comparing it with a fairly good imitation of itself, and it still won. If anything, that makes 0.2 a conservative floor on the specific effect rather than a ceiling. Then add that the benefit was still largely present a year later, which is not the usual behavior of an effect driven by expectation alone. Conclusion: a modest but real specific effect sitting on top of a substantial package effect, in a condition where the alternatives are long term medication with serious downsides. Offer it.

The theorist's reading. The claim under test was never just does the patient feel better. The claim was that qi circulates through channels, that specific points on those channels govern specific functions, and that inserting a needle at the correct point does something that inserting it elsewhere does not. That is a strong, specific, falsifiable claim about the body, and this study is one of the best tests of it ever run.

And what did the test find. A small specific advantage, and, far more tellingly, that the apparent size of acupuncture's benefit tracked what the comparison group got rather than how the acupuncture was done. If point selection and needling technique were carrying the mechanism, technique should have mattered. It did not stand out as the driver. That is exactly the pattern you would expect if the location of the needles were not the active component. And so, the theorist concludes, we have strong evidence that acupuncture treatment helps people and weak evidence for the account of why. The classical channel map may be a mnemonic, a tradition, a beautiful piece of intellectual architecture, and not a description of a real anatomical system.

Here is what I think, and you are free to disagree with me in your paper.

I think both readings are correct, because they are answering different questions, and this is precisely the situation chapter 1 was designed to prepare you for. Question three of our three questions, what does the evidence show compared with what, has two answers here because there were two comparisons. Question two, what is the proposed mechanism, has a different answer again. A treatment can be worth delivering while its explanation is wrong. That happens in conventional medicine too, more often than conventional medicine likes to advertise, and it is not a scandal. It is how medicine has usually worked: effective practice first, correct explanation later, sometimes much later.

But I also think the clinician cannot stop at so it does not matter why, and I want to be specific about why not, because this is where comfortable agnosticism becomes irresponsible.

If most of the benefit is non specific, several practical things follow. Training length and content come into question: if point precision is not the driver, what exactly are the years of training buying, and should they be spent differently. Cost comes into question, because patients pay for the specific claim. Scope comes into question, because the argument for needles in chronic pain does not automatically extend to needles for conditions where no comparable evidence exists, and the channel theory, if it is what is licensing the extension, is the weakest part of the structure. And the honest clinician has to face the possibility that other ways of delivering attention, time, touch and a coherent explanation might do about as well, which is a question nobody in any part of health care, mine included, especially wants to ask out loud.

One more caution, aimed at the clinician's argument, because I made it as strongly as I could and now I have to mark its weak point. The claim that the sham is not inert, and that 0.2 therefore understates the truth, is a reasonable claim and it is also dangerously flexible. Used carefully, it is a real methodological point about an active comparison. Used carelessly, it becomes a device for explaining away any disappointing result forever: the sham worked too well, so our treatment is better than it looks, every time, no matter what. Any argument that can absorb all possible evidence has stopped being evidence based. Make the point once, honestly, and then live with the number.

The mechanisms, and their actual standing

Since the mechanism question is where the theorist and the clinician part company, let me lay out the proposed mechanisms for acupuncture and tell you plainly what each one is and is not.

Before I do: none of what follows is established as the mechanism of clinical acupuncture. These are hypotheses with different amounts of support behind them, and the difference between a hypothesis and an established mechanism is the difference between a good idea and a demonstrated fact. I am telling you that in advance because almost every source you will read, including some respectable ones, presents one of these as settled.

Gate control. The underlying idea is that the spinal cord is not a passive wire. Input from large nerve fibers carrying touch, pressure and vibration can reduce the transmission of signals from the smaller fibers that carry pain. This is why rubbing a banged elbow helps. As a general feature of how pain is processed, this framework has substantial support and has shaped a great deal of pain science. Applying it to acupuncture specifically is an extrapolation: it offers a plausible route by which needling could reduce pain, and it conspicuously does not require the needle to be at a classical point. If anything, gate control predicts that needling nearly anywhere relevant would help, which fits the 0.2 rather better than it fits the channel map.

Endorphin release. The idea is that needling triggers release of the body's own opioid like molecules, which blunt pain. This has a plausible physiological basis and some experimental support. My pharmacology training makes me sympathetic to it and also cautious about it, because the same objection applies: a stress or stimulation driven release of endogenous opioids is not obviously point specific, and it is also one of the leading candidate explanations for placebo analgesia in general. A mechanism that explains both the treatment and its placebo is not much help in distinguishing them.

Connective tissue signaling. The idea is that rotating a needle in the fascia, the sheets of connective tissue under the skin, produces local mechanical deformation with downstream cellular and signaling consequences. This is the most speculative of the three in terms of clinical relevance. It is interesting, it is being worked on, and it has not been shown to account for clinical outcomes in patients.

And there is the classical account itself: needles at specified points regulate the circulation of qi through channels. Notice that this one is not in competition with the other three on equal terms, because it is making a claim of a different kind. The first three are attempts to describe acupuncture's effects in the vocabulary of modern physiology, which means they implicitly abandon the channel theory while trying to preserve the practice.

The intellectually honest summary is this. We have good evidence that acupuncture treatment reduces chronic pain. We have weak evidence for any particular account of why, including the traditional one. Those two sentences can be true at the same time and they both belong in your answer.

Safety is part of the evidence

I have put off the hardest material in this chapter until now, and I want to handle it carefully, because there are two ways to get this wrong and both of them hurt somebody.

If I soften this, some student finishes this course, recommends a product to her mother, and her mother is harmed. If I sensationalize it, some student concludes an entire medical tradition is poison, says so to a patient who has used it her whole life, and loses that patient's trust along with any chance of being told what she is actually taking. I am going to give you the study, exactly, and then tell you precisely what it does and does not support.

In 2004, researchers tested 70 ayurvedic herbal medicine products purchased from South Asian grocery stores in the Boston area. Fourteen of them, one in five, contained lead, mercury and/or arsenic.

Read the sampling description again, because it is the part people drop. These were products bought off the shelves of South Asian grocery stores in one American metropolitan area. Nearly all of the products tested were manufactured in South Asia, 26 in India and one in Pakistan. There was no United States manufactured comparison group in this study. None. That matters enormously for what you are allowed to conclude, and I will come back to it in a moment.

First, why the finding is serious. Lead, mercury and arsenic are not trace curiosities. Lead is a neurotoxin, and it is worst in exactly the populations most likely to be given a gentle traditional remedy: pregnant women and young children. Its effects on the developing nervous system occur at low exposures and are not reversible. Mercury and arsenic are each toxic through their own mechanisms. These are not substances where a small amount is fine because it is natural. They have no nutritional role. One in five of the products on those shelves contained at least one of them.

Why might a herbal product contain a heavy metal at all? Two explanations are usually offered, and the study does not resolve which applies to which product. The first is contamination: metals taken up from soil or water where plants were grown, or introduced during processing, storage or packaging. The second is deliberate inclusion. As I mentioned when describing the tradition, some categories of classical ayurvedic preparation incorporate processed minerals and metals as intended ingredients, within an elaborate preparatory procedure that is held to transform them. A product made in that tradition would contain metal by design. I am not going to tell you which explanation accounts for the fourteen products, because the study does not say, and I am not going to guess in a book that students will cite.

Now what this study does not support, and here I need you to be as disciplined as I am asking you to be anywhere in the course.

It does not support the claim that imported ayurvedic products are more contaminated than American made ones. It cannot support that claim, because American made ones were not tested. There is no comparison group. If you leave this chapter saying buy domestic, you have invented a finding, and you have done it in exactly the direction that flatters an existing assumption, which is how most invented findings happen.

It does not tell you that four in five of those products were safe in every other respect. Not containing lead, mercury or arsenic is not the same as being pure, correctly labeled, or appropriately dosed. The study tested for what it tested for.

It does not tell you about today's market. This is a 2004 study. Regulation, sourcing and testing may have improved since, or not, and this study cannot tell you which. Do not cite it as a description of what is on a shelf in 2026. Cite it for what it is: a documented demonstration that a fifth of a real sample of real products being sold to real families contained heavy metals, which is enough to destroy any assumption that these products are inherently safe.

And it does not tell you anything about ayurvedic practice as a whole. A practitioner prescribing a regimen of food, sleep schedule and oil massage is not doing the thing this study found. The finding is about products, which are one part of a large system, and the part most likely to be bought by someone with no practitioner involved at all.

What I want you to take from this is a sentence I will be repeating for the rest of the course. Natural and safe are different words. They are not even the same kind of word. Natural is a claim about where something came from. Safe is a claim about what it does to a body at a given dose. Nothing about the first implies the second. I spent several years in a pharmacology program learning dose response relationships, and the lesson that stuck hardest is that every substance that can do something useful at one dose can do something harmful at another, and that plants are a particularly difficult case because the dose in a plant product varies with the plant, the soil, the season, the part used, the preparation and the batch. A tablet of a licensed drug has a number on it. A spoonful of a herbal preparation usually does not.

There is a second safety issue with any plant medicine, and it comes from the same pharmacology, so let me state it generally rather than with specific examples I cannot source for you. Plant products contain active molecules. Active molecules are handled by the same liver enzyme systems that handle prescription drugs, and they can compete with those drugs, speed up their clearance or slow it down. The patients where this matters most are the ones on medications with a narrow margin between a useful dose and a dangerous one. The clinical implication is not that patients should stop using traditional preparations. It is that their clinicians need to know what they are taking, by name and by brand, which brings us back to chapter 1.

Because here is the practical conclusion, and it is the opposite of what students usually expect from a safety section. The useful response to this evidence is not to tell patients these products are dangerous. It is to be the kind of clinician they will tell. A patient who expects contempt will not mention the preparation her aunt sends from Delhi. If she does not mention it, nobody checks it, nobody considers it when her child's lead level comes back odd, and nobody thinks about it when her anticoagulant stops behaving. Contempt does not stop the exposure. It hides it. Ask, without a face, every time.

One more note for completeness, and it is a note about a gap rather than a finding. People will ask you whether acupuncture is safe. I have no checked figure in this book's fact base on rates of adverse events from acupuncture, and I am therefore not going to give you one, because a number I supplied from memory would be exactly the kind of thing this book exists to argue against. What I can tell you is the structure of the question: the risks of inserting a thin solid object through skin concern sterility, depth and anatomy, and they are addressed, in principle, by single use sterile needles and by training in where not to go. Whether they are addressed in practice, and at what rate, is an empirical question with an answer I do not have in front of me. Go and look it up.

Running the three questions on a whole system

Let me pull this together by doing, out loud, what I am going to ask you to do on the exam.

What is the claim? For TCM: that health consists in the free and balanced movement of qi within an organized system of functional organs and channels, that disturbance of that movement produces identifiable patterns, that a trained practitioner can read those patterns through looking, listening, asking and palpating, and that needles, heat, manual therapy and modifiable herbal formulas can correct them. For ayurveda: that each person has a constitutional proportion of three functional principles, that illness is departure from that proportion, often beginning with impaired digestive capacity and the accumulation of undigested residue, and that food, routine, herbs and periodic purification can restore it.

What is the proposed mechanism? In both cases, the mechanism is internal to the system's own physiology. That is the distinctive feature of a whole system and the source of all the difficulty. The mechanism is not a hypothesis stated in the vocabulary of the thing being explained to an outsider. It is qi, and channels, and doshas, and agni. When modern researchers propose gate control or endorphin release, they are not testing the system's mechanism. They are proposing a replacement for it that would preserve the practice.

What does the evidence show, compared with what? For acupuncture in chronic pain, we have an unusually strong answer: a moderate benefit of about 0.5 standard deviations compared with no acupuncture, a small benefit of about 0.2 standard deviations compared with sham, across 39 trials and 20,827 patients, persisting at one year with about a 15 percent decline, with effect size tracking the control condition rather than the technique. For the rest of both systems, taken as delivered systems, we do not have anything approaching that quality, and I will say more about that in a moment.

Notice what the three questions did here. They did not deliver a verdict. They separated a strong empirical result from a weakly supported theory, and they prevented the strength of the first from being transferred to the second. That transfer, from this works to therefore the explanation is right, is the single most common error in writing about this field, and it is made just as often in reverse, from the explanation is wrong to therefore it does not work. You now have the equipment to refuse both.

Where the evidence runs out

This chapter has more holes in it than any so far, and some of them are large. Here they are, named.

I cannot tell you how many Americans use ayurveda or Traditional Chinese medicine. You will find figures for this in all sorts of places. I do not have one that meets the standard of this book, so I am not giving you one. Compare that with chapter 1, where I handed you figures for overall use, for yoga, for meditation and for chiropractic with confidence intervals attached. The contrast is the point: some things in this field are well counted and some are not, and the ones that are not tend to get the most confident numbers attached to them.

I cannot tell you whether the diagnostic procedures are reliable. I raised this earlier and I am raising it again because it deserves to be the thing you remember from this section. Pulse reading and pattern differentiation make testable claims about what a trained practitioner can perceive and categorize, and the foundation of both systems rests on those claims. I have no checked data on how well practitioners agree with each other. It is a researchable question. It is arguably the most important researchable question about either system, more important than another trial of needles for knees, because a system whose diagnoses are not reproducible cannot deliver the individualization that is its central claim.

I cannot give you a whole system result for either tradition that is comparable in quality to the acupuncture result. This is the asymmetry I most want you to notice. The cleanest number in this book is for a single extracted component of one of these systems, studied in the way that single components can be studied. For ayurveda as practiced, a practitioner reading a constitution and prescribing diet, routine, herbs and purification, and for TCM as practiced, a practitioner differentiating a pattern and prescribing a modified formula alongside needles, I have no result of that standard to show you. That absence is not proof that those systems do not work. It is an absence, and it is partly a consequence of the methodological problem I spent several pages on, and it is also partly a consequence of where research money has gone. All three of those things are true and you should not collapse them into each other.

I cannot resolve the mechanism question and neither can anyone else right now. Gate control, endorphin release and connective tissue signaling are candidates of differing support. The classical account is a candidate too, and it is the one the 39 trials gave the least help to. We do not know, and saying we do not know is not a polite way of saying it is nonsense.

I cannot tell you how much of today's practice is continuous with the classical texts. I raised the antiquity question earlier and left it open, and it is still open. The history is genuinely contested, the reorganizations of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries were real and substantial, and a careful historian would want to go tradition by tradition, region by region, rather than issue a verdict. What I can tell you is that the question is a historical one and that neither answer settles anything about whether a treatment helps a patient.

And I cannot tell you what is in the bottle on the shelf today. The heavy metal study is from 2004, it sampled grocery stores in one city, it had no domestic comparison group, and it is the best evidence I have to show you. That is a thin basis for the strength of the warning I have given you, and I have given the warning anyway, because the cost of being wrong in the reassuring direction falls on a child with a lead level, and the cost of being wrong in the cautious direction falls on an adult who buys a slightly more expensive product. Those costs are not symmetrical. When evidence is thin and the harms are lopsided, you lean toward the side where being wrong is cheaper, and you say out loud that that is what you are doing. That is not a scientific judgment. It is a clinical one, and clinicians make it every day.

Chapter 6

Nature, Place and Daily Life

What do you do with a large pile of studies that all find something and none of them can rule out the obvious objection?

The thing I recommend most often is the thing I can defend with the fewest numbers.

Sit with how uncomfortable that sentence is, because it is true of me and it will be true of you. Five days a week I tell people to walk. Not as a formality at the end of a visit, but deliberately, with specifics, because I believe it matters. I tell them to walk outside rather than on a treadmill if they have the option. I ask about their sleep and I ask who they eat dinner with. None of that is chiropractic. None of it is in the technique I was trained in. It is the part of the visit I would least want to give up, and it is the part I have the thinnest evidence for.

Meanwhile the chapters behind us had numbers in them. Chapter 5 gave you 39 trials and 20,827 patients and a gap between 0.5 and 0.2 that taught you more about this field than any argument I could make. Chapter 4 gave you 47 randomized trials and effect sizes carried out to two decimal places. Those chapters had the opposite problem from this one. There I had to teach you that a well measured small effect beats a confident story.

This chapter is the other problem, and in practice it is the more common one. Here the research is not scarce. There is a great deal of it. Much of it finds something. People who spend more time around trees and grass report better mood and lower stress and sleep better and in some studies live longer. The studies keep coming and they keep pointing the same direction, and almost none of them can rule out the objection a skeptical undergraduate would raise in about four seconds, which is that people who spend time in forests are different from people who do not in roughly a hundred ways, and that the trees might have nothing to do with it.

That objection has a name, confounding, and teaching it in plain language is the main work of this chapter. A confounder is a third thing, attached both to the habit you are studying and to the outcome you are measuring, which could produce the connection between them all by itself. Once you can see confounders, you will see them everywhere, and for about a week you will be insufferable at parties. Then it settles into something more useful: not a reflex for dismissing research, but a way of saying precisely what a given study is and is not able to tell you.

Because here is the move I most want you to avoid. Students who learn about confounding for the first time often conclude that observational research is worthless, that only randomized trials count, and that therefore we know nothing about nature or sleep or diet or friendship. That is the wrong lesson, it is lazier than the enthusiasm it replaces, and acting on it would make you a worse clinician. Confounding does not turn a finding into garbage. It changes what kind of claim the finding supports, and your job is to say which kind. That is harder than approving or dismissing, and it is the whole skill.

The usual housekeeping. These are not my traditions. I have never trained as a forest therapy guide, I do not run a sleep clinic, and I am not a nutritionist. Everything I say about these approaches comes from the literature and from watching what happens to patients who change their habits, which is clinical observation and not evidence, and I will label it as such when I use it. There is one exception to my outsider status here, and it cuts against me rather than for me: I am a practitioner who hands out lifestyle advice daily, which means I have a personal stake in believing it works. Read this chapter the way I asked you to read chapter 3.

Forest bathing and what is claimed for it

Run the first question. What is the claim, stated the way a believer would state it, at its strongest?

Forest bathing is the usual English rendering of a Japanese term, shinrin yoku, ordinarily translated as something like taking in the atmosphere of the forest. The word bathing is not about water. It is about immersion: you put your whole sensing body into a wooded place and let it be worked on. You will read in many popular articles that the term was coined by a particular Japanese government agency in a particular year. I am not going to give you that date, because I have not checked it against a source I trust, and this book does not hand you facts I have only heard. What I can tell you is that the practice as it now travels internationally is recent in its organized form, with guides, certifications and marketed retreats, and that its promoters present it as the formalization of something older and more ordinary than itself.

The practice, in its disciplined version, is not a hike, and practitioners are insistent about the distinction. A hike has a destination, a pace and a distance, and attention goes to the trail and the time. Forest bathing deliberately removes all three. A session might cover a few hundred meters in two hours. A guide slows the group down, invites attention to one sense at a time, asks people to notice what is moving, what is making noise, what the air feels like on skin. There is often no talking for long stretches. The instruction is not to achieve anything, and the framing is that the forest is doing something to you rather than you doing something in the forest.

Stated at its strongest, the claim is this. Human beings evolved in and among living landscapes, our senses were built for them, and the environments most of us now inhabit, which are enclosed, loud, lit artificially, saturated with screens and demanding of continuous directed attention, impose a low grade load on us that we have stopped noticing because it never lets up. Putting a person back into a living landscape, slowly and attentively, lifts that load. The body settles. Attention that has been held tight all week goes loose and wide. Stress physiology comes down. And because so much of chronic illness has a stress component, that settling is not a luxury, it is medicine, and it is medicine that costs nothing and has essentially no side effects.

That claim deserves to be stated before it is tested. Notice two features of it. First, it is as much a claim about a thing being absent, the load, as about a thing being present, the trees, and that will matter when we reach the evidence, because an absence is hard to attribute. Second, nearly everyone finds it immediately plausible, including me and including the most hard nosed skeptic in any medical school. That plausibility is the chapter's central trap. Plausible is not demonstrated. Some of the most catastrophic mistakes in the history of medicine were extremely plausible.

Alongside forest bathing, and much larger than it, sits a broader research literature usually filed under green space. Green space just means vegetated land that people can reach or see: parks, street trees, playing fields, gardens, woodland at the edge of a city, sometimes merely the view from a window. The green space literature is mostly not about a practice at all. It is about where people live. Researchers take a measure of how much vegetation surrounds a person's address, often from satellite imagery, and relate it to that person's health. No one is being asked to do anything. Nobody is bathing in anything. The claim being tested is about exposure rather than practice, and it is closer to a claim about city planning than a claim about therapy.

Keep those two apart for the rest of this chapter, because they are constantly collapsed together in popular writing and they are not the same object. Forest bathing is a guided practice with an intention. Green space is a feature of an address. When a wellness article cites a satellite imagery study about neighborhood vegetation to support a paid weekend forest therapy retreat, there is a step missing in the middle, and it is the same missing step you saw in chapter 4 when trial evidence about a taught eight week program was used to sell a phone application.

The mechanism stories, and how much weight each bears

Second question. What is the proposed mechanism, the story about why it works?

There are four stories in general circulation, and they are of very different quality. I want to lay them out in order, strongest to weakest, because sorting mechanism stories by how much weight they can bear is a skill in itself and this is a good place to practice it.

The first story is the least exciting and the most solid, and it is usually skipped. It is that a forest walk is a walk. It involves getting out of a chair, moving for an extended period, being in daylight, usually being away from a screen, and often being with another person. Every one of those is a plausible route to feeling better, and not one of them has anything to do with trees. This is the story a skeptic tells, and the thing to notice is that the skeptic's story here is not a debunking. It is a mechanism. It says the effect is real and the explanation is mundane.

The second story is about attention. The proposal is that modern environments demand effortful, directed attention almost continuously, that this capacity depletes, and that natural settings hold attention gently and involuntarily rather than demanding it, which allows the effortful kind to recover. You can feel the appeal of this immediately, which is both its strength and its danger. It is also at least partly testable, which puts it ahead of most of the mechanism stories in this book.

The third story is physiological. Forest exposure is said to shift the balance of the autonomic nervous system, the part of the nervous system that runs the body without your involvement, away from the activated state and toward the settled one, with downstream effects on heart rate, blood pressure and stress hormones. This is a reasonable hypothesis, measurements of this kind have been taken, and I am going to be careful here for a reason I will explain in detail shortly: measurements taken immediately after a pleasant walk in people who know they are in a study of pleasant walks are hard to interpret.

The fourth story is the one you see most often in marketing copy, and it is the weakest. It holds that trees release airborne compounds, often called phytoncides, that are inhaled by walkers and act on human immune function. It has the pleasing shape of a real pharmacological mechanism, which is why it travels so well in a wellness newsletter. Having spent several years learning how molecules change what bodies do, here is what my training makes me want before I believe a story like that: which compound, at what concentration in forest air, absorbed by what route, reaching what tissue at what dose, acting on what receptor, producing what measured change, in a design that could tell the compound apart from the walk. I do not have that chain of evidence to show you. Parts of it may exist and I am not asserting otherwise. I am telling you that this book does not let me hand you a mechanism dressed as a finding.

That is worth a general rule. The more biochemical a mechanism story sounds, the more it borrows the authority of a discipline that may never have signed off on it. Phytoncides, cortisol, immune boosting and inflammation are real words in real sciences, routinely used in wellness writing as decoration rather than as claims anyone intends to defend. When you meet one, ask what measurement would have to exist for the sentence to be true, then ask whether you have been shown it.

Lifestyle approaches, and why they are different

Now widen the frame, because forest bathing is a small member of a much bigger family, and the bigger family is where you will spend your working life.

The lifestyle approaches are sleep, movement, diet and social connection. Everyone in holistic health recommends them. Everyone in conventional medicine recommends them too, which is the first thing that makes them strange company for the traditions in this book. Go back to chapter 1 and the federal definitions, which turn on whether an approach is non mainstream and whether it is used with conventional care or in place of it. Walking is not non mainstream. Neither is sleeping eight hours or eating vegetables or having friends. A cardiologist will tell you to do all four, and will write it in the chart, and will not think of herself as practicing complementary medicine while she does it.

So why are they in this book at all? Three reasons, and they are the reasons this chapter exists.

The first is the one I argued in chapter 1. Holistic is not a synonym for alternative. It is a stance toward the patient, the claim that a person comes as a package and that treating one part while ignoring the rest is bad practice. The lifestyle approaches are what that stance looks like when somebody actually acts on it. Conventional medicine agrees with the stance and is structured so as to make acting on it nearly impossible: a fifteen minute visit will not get you a useful conversation about a patient's sleep, let alone her marriage or her commute or whether there is a grocery store she can reach. The holistic traditions did not discover sleep and vegetables. What they did, often, was build practices with enough time in them to take sleep and vegetables seriously. The criticism was never that the regulars denied the whole person. It was that the system had no slot for one. That remains the most substantial thing the holistic traditions have to say, and it is very nearly the only one of their claims that almost nobody disputes.

The second reason is that the holistic traditions package these four things in ways that carry extra claims riding along. Ayurveda puts daily routine, food and the timing of meals at the center of its system, inside a framework of constitution and dosha that makes individual prescriptions the whole point. Yoga is presented by its serious teachers not as a back exercise but as a comprehensive discipline covering conduct, breath, diet and the shape of ordinary life. In both cases the lifestyle core is uncontroversial and the framework around it is where the argument lives. Separating those two layers is something you will do constantly, and if you cannot do it you will either swallow the framework to get the advice or throw out the advice to be rid of the framework.

The third reason is the one that will matter most to you professionally, and it is why the last third of this chapter is what it is. These are the approaches you are most likely to actually recommend. Not acupuncture, which requires a licensed provider and a payment. Not an ayurvedic preparation, where chapter 5 left you with a real warning about what is in the bottle. Sleep, movement, food, friendship and time outdoors are cheap or free, broadly safe, available without a gatekeeper, and easy to begin this afternoon. Whatever field you end up in, these will be in your mouth more than anything else in this book. Which means the quality of your honesty about them is not an academic matter. It is most of what your patients will get from you.

And here I have to tell you something awkward about this book. I am not going to give you numbers for sleep, diet, movement or social connection. You have noticed by now that I give you a figure whenever I have one that has been checked, with its confidence interval where the source reports one, and that I refuse to give you a figure when I do not. I do not have checked figures for these four. The literatures are enormous, I have read in them, and I am not going to summarize them from memory, because a number recalled rather than looked up is exactly the failure this book treats as unforgivable. So what you will get from me on the lifestyle approaches is reasoning, not arithmetic, and you should notice that the reasoning is strong enough to act on anyway. That is not a cop out. It is a demonstration of something you will need: how to make a defensible recommendation when you do not have a number in your pocket.

I will give you one checked number that cuts against the easy story, because it is in our fact base and because it is salutary. The meditation review from chapter 4, the one with 47 randomized trials and active controls, found moderate evidence of benefit for anxiety, depression and pain. For sleep it found something different: low evidence of no effect, or insufficient evidence of any effect. The same for eating habits and for weight. So here is a program that plausibly should help people sleep, that is marketed on exactly that promise, and the best review of it, held to a decent standard of comparison, could not show that it does. Plausibility is cheap. Keep that result next to every intuition you have about lifestyle, including the ones in this chapter, including mine.

Observational evidence and its ceiling

Third question, the one that runs this course. What does the evidence show, compared with what?

The anchor for this chapter is a systematic review by Oh, Lee, Zaslawski and colleagues, published in 2017 in Environmental Health and Preventive Medicine, on the health and well being benefits of spending time in forests. A systematic review, as you know from chapter 4, means the authors searched the literature by a stated method, decided in advance what to include, and summarized the result.

I am going to handle this review differently from the two anchors in chapter 4, and I will be open about why. Our fact base carries an explicit caution attached to this citation: read the designs of the included studies before making any claim about effect size, because the literature is largely observational, or small and short term, and the obvious confounders are rarely controlled. I am honoring that caution. So I will not give you an effect size for nature exposure anywhere in this chapter. Not a number, not a rough equivalent, not a comparison to the 0.33 you already have for meditation and pain. You will notice the absence, and the absence is the lesson.

Compare that with the rest of this book. I gave you 0.5 and 0.2 for acupuncture against two different controls, and 0.38 and 0.30 and 0.33 for meditation at eight weeks, falling to 0.22 and 0.23 at 3 to 6 months. In each case I could tell you the size of the thing because somebody had measured it in a design capable of supporting a measurement. Here I cannot, and a book that gave you a number anyway, so that this chapter would look like the others, would be lying to you in the most ordinary way health writing lies.

So let me teach you the designs instead, because once you know them you can read any study in this area and know its ceiling before you reach the results.

A cross sectional study measures a group of people once. It asks how much green space surrounds their homes and how they are doing, at the same moment, and reports whether the two line up. It is the cheapest design and the weakest. It cannot tell you which came first, and it cannot tell you anything about change.

A cohort study follows a group forward over time. It records something about people now, green space near the address, say, or self reported time outdoors, and watches what happens over years. This is better, because the exposure is recorded before the outcome, which rules out the simplest version of the arrow running backwards. It is also slow and still observational, which is the word that matters. Observational means nobody assigned anyone to anything. Whoever ended up in the leafy neighborhood got there by the ordinary processes of money, work, family and history, not by a coin flip.

A randomized trial assigns people by chance. That is the only thing randomization does, and it is why the whole apparatus of modern clinical evidence rests on it. When assignment is random, the two groups are on average alike in everything, including the hundred things nobody thought to measure, so a difference at the end can be attributed to the thing that was assigned. Randomization does not make a study good. But it is the only tool we have for removing the confounders we do not know about, and nothing else on the list even attempts that.

Now apply this to nature. Can you randomize it? Partly, and only in the small. You can take sixty volunteers, send half on a guided walk in woodland and half on a matched walk along a road, and measure mood and blood pressure afterward. People have done that sort of study, and it is a genuine attempt at the right comparison, which is walking in nature against walking somewhere else rather than nature against nothing. Hold that design up against the ladder from chapter 4 and you will see it is reaching for the non specific active control rung, the same rung that produced meditation's best numbers, and that is to its credit.

But look at what it cannot do. It lasts an afternoon. The claim it is being used to support is about the shape of a life. The participants know perfectly well which group they are in, because you cannot blind a person to the presence of trees, and no sham forest is possible for exactly the reasons no sham yoga is possible. The outcome is often a questionnaire filled in minutes after a pleasant experience by someone who understands that the researchers hope the pleasant experience helped. And the people who volunteer for a study about forest walks are not a random sample of humanity, they are people who like forests. Every one of those limitations is the kind the Cochrane reviewers in chapter 4 would list as a reason to downgrade certainty, and they would be right.

What you cannot do, in any form, is randomize the big version. You cannot assign a thousand families at birth to grow up with woodland behind the house and a thousand to grow up with none, and follow them for forty years. Nobody will ever run that study. It would be the only design capable of answering the question people actually want answered, and it is permanently unavailable. That is not a temporary gap waiting on funding. It is a ceiling, and it is the same kind of permanent ceiling you met with sham yoga, arriving for a different reason.

So the honest summary is this. There is a lot of this literature. Much of it finds associations in the direction the enthusiasts would predict. The strongest designs are short, small and unblinded. The designs that run long enough to matter are observational. And the two halves cannot be added together to make one solid conclusion, however much the word systematic in front of review makes it feel as though they can.

Confounding, in plain language

Here is the objection, and here is the machinery for handling it.

Imagine two women, both 54, both living in the same mid sized city. Diane walks in a wooded park near her house three or four mornings a week and has done for years. Patrice does not. We measure both of them on anything you like: mood, blood pressure, sleep, how many days of work they missed last year, how long they live. Suppose Diane does better, as she probably will, and as the research would predict.

Now list the ways Diane and Patrice differ, other than the park.

Diane lives within walking distance of a wooded park, which in most American cities is a fact about property values. Her neighborhood likely has better air, less traffic noise, fewer industrial neighbors and a longer history of public investment. She can walk in the morning, which means her work starts later or she sets her own hours or she does not work. She is well enough to walk for forty five minutes on uneven ground, which excludes a long list of conditions, some of them conditions Patrice may have. She feels safe walking alone at dawn, which is partly about the neighborhood and partly about her history. She has forty five unclaimed minutes, which means nobody is depending on her at that hour, which means she is probably not caring for a parent or a small child alone. She is the sort of person who does a health promoting thing consistently for years, and people like that usually do several other health promoting things consistently for years. She has a car that starts, or does not need one. She has shoes.

Every item on that list is a candidate confounder. A confounder, again, is a third thing attached both to the exposure and to the outcome, capable of producing the association on its own. Income is attached to park access and attached to health. Mobility is attached to walking and attached to health. Free time is attached to both. Conscientiousness is attached to both. Not one of those is the trees, and all of them would produce exactly the pattern the study found if the trees did nothing whatsoever.

There is a second problem hiding inside the first, and it is sharper. The arrow may run the other way. We have been assuming walking makes Diane well. Consider that being well lets Diane walk. A person with a painful knee, a depression, a breathing problem or a crushing fatigue does not go for dawn walks in the park, and all four of those conditions will also show up in the outcome column. So a study finding that walkers are healthier may have discovered nothing more than that healthy people walk. This is called reverse causation, and it is particularly vicious in this literature, because almost every outcome anyone measures, mood, sleep, energy, pain, is also a reason a person would or would not go outside. A cohort design, which measures the exposure before the outcome, cuts the crudest form of this. It does not cut the subtle form, where an illness was already developing quietly and had already stopped the walking before anyone filled in a form.

What do researchers do about all this? They adjust, which is also called controlling for. The plain language version is this: you use statistics to compare people who are similar on the confounder. Rather than comparing all walkers with all non walkers, you compare walkers and non walkers at the same income, then at the next income, and so on, and combine the results. If the association survives, you have shown that income alone does not explain it.

Adjustment is a real and valuable tool and it is not nothing. It is also, in this literature, systematically oversold, for three reasons you should be able to recite.

First, you can only adjust for what you measured. Nobody measured whether Diane has forty five unclaimed minutes in her morning, or how safe she feels on her street, or how conscientious she is, or whether her mother is ill. These are not exotic variables. They are the obvious ones, and they are almost never in the data set.

Second, you can only adjust for it as well as you measured it. Income in a large study is usually a band, five or six brackets. Education is usually a highest degree. Those are crude stand ins for the thing that actually matters, which is the whole texture of a person's circumstances, and adjusting for a crude version of a confounder removes only part of its influence. What is left over is called residual confounding, and it is the ghost in every one of these papers.

Third, and this is the part that surprises people, adjustment can be actively wrong. If you adjust for something that sits on the path between the exposure and the outcome, you subtract part of the effect you were trying to measure. Suppose time outdoors improves sleep and better sleep improves mood. If you adjust for sleep while studying mood, you will make the nature effect look smaller than it is. There is no formula that tells you which variables to adjust for. That judgment comes from a theory about how the world works, and reasonable researchers disagree.

Put all three together and you get the honest statement about an adjusted observational finding. It is not raw. Some obvious alternatives have been reduced. It has not been cleaned, and the people who did the work generally say so in their limitations paragraph, which is the paragraph nobody quotes.

Let me put this in a clinic. A patient of mine, Ray, is 58 and works the floor at a hardware store, which means nine hours a day on concrete and a lower back and right knee that ache most evenings. He read something about forest bathing and wants to know whether it is real. Ray is not asking me an epistemology question. He is asking whether to spend his Saturday morning on it.

The confounding analysis I just walked you through is exactly what makes Ray's question hard, and here is the uncomfortable detail. Many of the confounders I listed for Diane are things Ray does not have. He does not have a wooded park within walking distance. He does not have an unclaimed morning. He is not well enough to walk comfortably for forty five minutes, which is in fact why he came to see me. So even if every study in this literature were perfectly clean, I would have to ask whether it applied to him, because the people in those studies had the very advantages Ray lacks. Confounding is not only a reason a finding might be false. It is also a reason a true finding might not transfer to the person in front of you. Both of those are what we mean when we say observational evidence has a ceiling.

A different kind of claim, not a worthless one

Now the turn, because if I left you where the last section ended I would have taught you a sneer.

Confounding does not make a finding worthless. It changes the kind of claim the finding supports. Here are the kinds, in order, and I want you to be able to name which one you are holding.

This exists in the world. People who have more contact with green space tend to do better on various measures of health and well being, and this shows up repeatedly, in different countries, with different methods, by different research groups. That is a real finding about the world, and it is not undone by confounding, because it does not claim to explain itself. It is a pattern. Patterns are worth knowing. A pattern this consistent is where every good causal question starts.

This has a plausible mechanism. There are candidate explanations of differing quality, as we sorted out earlier, ranging from the mundane and robust to the decorative. A mechanism story raises a claim's standing a little. It does not establish it. Chapter 5 should have broken you of the habit of treating a mechanism as evidence, since the acupuncture result is the strongest number in this book and the classical mechanism it was supposed to demonstrate was the thing the trials gave least help to.

This causes that. Doing more of it will make you better off than you would otherwise have been. This is the claim everyone wants and the claim this literature cannot currently deliver at the scale that matters, for the structural reasons above.

This much of it produces this much benefit. A dose and an effect size. This is the claim I am forbidden from giving you here, correctly, and it is also the claim that wellness marketing makes most freely, which should tell you something about the relationship between confidence and knowledge.

Say those out loud as four sentences and notice something: the first is already enough to act on in some circumstances. Not all. But some. A pattern plus a mundane mechanism plus near zero cost plus near zero risk is a respectable basis for a suggestion, as long as you say it is a suggestion. The error is not acting on tier one evidence. The error is describing tier one evidence in tier four language. Almost everything wrong with how this field talks about itself is that single substitution, and the rest of this chapter is about how not to make it.

How to recommend something honestly

So we arrive at the practical part, and I have arranged the chapter so that it gets the space it deserves, because for most of you this is the part of the book you will use next week.

Start with why these approaches are the ones that will actually come out of your mouth. Four features, and they are worth being explicit about.

They are cheap or free. A walk costs nothing, and going to bed earlier costs nothing. Compare that with acupuncture, which gave us the best evidence in this book and also requires a licensed practitioner, a schedule and a payment every visit.

They are broadly safe. I say broadly rather than entirely, and I will come back to it.

They need no gatekeeper. Nobody has to refer, authorize, prescribe or bill. Whatever your scope of practice, you may tell someone to go outside.

They are easy to start. The first dose can be this afternoon, which matters more than it sounds, because the gap between intending and beginning is where most health advice dies.

Those four features change how much evidence you need, and the argument has to be made carefully, because it can be abused in one sentence.

How much evidence a recommendation requires depends on what the recommendation costs, what it risks, and what it displaces. That is not a scientific principle. It is a clinical one, and I made the same move at the end of chapter 5 when I told you that when evidence is thin and the possible harms are lopsided, you lean toward the side where being wrong is cheaper, and you say out loud that that is what you are doing. A recommendation that costs nothing, risks almost nothing and displaces nothing has a low bar. A recommendation that costs a patient a thousand dollars, or replaces a medication, or occupies the months in which a treatable disease progresses, has a very high one.

Now the abuse, so you can see it coming. The sentence I just wrote is not weak evidence is fine for cheap things. It does not license you to recommend anything at all provided it is inexpensive. Three constraints hold it in place.

The bar scales with the claim, not just with the cost. Suggesting a walk in the park on tier one evidence is reasonable. Telling a patient that the walk will lower her blood pressure by a specific amount is not reasonable on tier one evidence, no matter how cheap the walk is. The price of the intervention does not buy you a stronger claim about it.

Cheap is not the same as free. A patient has a finite budget of attention, effort and willingness. Every thing you tell her to do spends some of it. Hand a person seven lifestyle changes and you have not given her seven chances to improve, you have given her a reason to stop listening. I would rather a patient left my office with one change she will make than five she will nod at.

And the thing to be most careful about is displacement, which is chapter 1 arriving again. The moment a cheap safe recommendation becomes a reason not to do something that would have worked, its evidence bar jumps to the top of the ladder. The nature walk is complementary when it is added to care and alternative when it replaces it, and nothing about the walk changed. I have watched a man tell himself his chest discomfort was stress and that he needed to get outside more, and he was both right about the stress and three weeks late to a cardiologist. The recommendation did not hurt him. His use of it did. A recommendation is not finished until you have said what it does not replace.

Which brings me to the word safe, and to being honest about broadly. Outdoor activity carries ordinary outdoor risks. Heat, cold, sun, insects and the illnesses they carry, allergies, falls on uneven ground, poor air quality on bad days, and in some neighborhoods a genuine question of personal safety. For a patient with a cardiac condition, a balance problem, a respiratory disease or a wound that must stay clean, outside is not automatically neutral. I have no study to hand you that sizes any of those risks and I am not going to imply they are negligible because they are unglamorous. What I will say is that they are the sort of risk a reasonable adult can be told about in one sentence and then judge for herself, which is a different category from the risks in chapter 5, where the thing in the bottle was not on the label and the patient could not have assessed it. Scaring a person off something safe and reassuring her about something that is not are both harms, and you will be tempted by each.

What you can promise and what you cannot

Here is the division, and I have put it in the form I actually use, which is three buckets.

What I can say flatly. That people who do more of this tend to report doing better. That it is cheap. That for most people it is safe, with the ordinary cautions. That it will not interact with your medications. That you can find out in a month whether it does anything for you specifically. That nothing about trying it closes off anything else. Every one of those is either a tier one finding stated as a tier one finding, or a statement about cost and risk rather than about benefit, and I can defend all of them.

What I can say with the hedge attached and spoken aloud. That it may help your sleep or your mood. That many of my patients tell me it helps them, and that I am telling you what I have noticed rather than what has been proven. That I think it is worth your Saturday morning. Clinical observation is allowed in a consultation. It is allowed because it is sometimes the only thing available, and it is honest only if labeled. The phrase I use is some version of here is what I see in my practice, which is not the same as here is what the research shows.

What I cannot say at all. Any number. That it will lower your blood pressure or your stress hormones by any amount. That it boosts your immune system. That it is clinically proven, which is a phrase with no definition and therefore no honest use. That it will prevent any disease. That two hours a week is the right dose, because nobody has established a dose. And I cannot tell a patient that nature specifically did the work, as opposed to the walking, the daylight, the quiet or the company, because the designs capable of separating those are short, small and few.

Notice that the third bucket holds every sentence that would make the recommendation sound impressive. That is no coincidence. Those sentences are impressive precisely because they are tier four claims, and tier four is the tier we do not have.

Now the objection every student raises here, and it is fair. If I strip out the impressive sentences, will the patient still do it? Does honesty cost compliance?

Sometimes, a little. I think the trade is still worth taking, for a reason that is practical rather than moral. Oversold advice has a short half life. A patient told that forest walks will transform her sleep, who walks for three weeks and sleeps the same, does not conclude that she is an exception. She concludes that you talk nonsense, and she files everything else you have ever told her under the same heading. Worse, she stops telling you things. Chapter 1 gave you the figure that matters here: more than one adult in three used a complementary approach in 2022, and patients routinely conceal it from clinicians because they expect to be mocked. The clinician who oversells and the clinician who sneers both end up with the same patient, the one who no longer tells the truth about what she is taking, and that patient is less safe for it. Your credibility is a clinical instrument. Spending it on a sentence you cannot defend is a bad trade at any price.

Saying it so a patient will act on it

Honest is not enough. Honest and unusable is a failure too, and it is the failure academics make. A patient does not act on a qualified statement about tiers of evidence. She acts on a sentence she can repeat to herself on a Tuesday. So the last skill here is putting accuracy into language that moves someone.

Four tools.

Choose the verb deliberately. Will, may and tend to are three different claims and patients hear the difference even when they cannot name it. This will help you sleep is a promise I cannot keep. This may help your sleep, and a lot of people find it does, is true and still carries momentum. People who get outside more tend to sleep better, and we are not certain why, is the most accurate of the three and the least motivating, which is why I usually put it second rather than first. Do not reach for will. Reaching for will is how clinicians lie without noticing.

Name the smallest version that fits the actual life. This is the one most often skipped and it does the most work. Ray does not have forty five minutes in a wooded park. He has a fifteen minute break and there are three trees and a strip of grass behind the loading dock. So the recommendation is not go forest bathing. It is on your break, go stand outside by the grass instead of in the break room, and sit down out there if you can. That is a real recommendation that fits a real day, and it is calibrated to exactly the evidence I have, which concerns ordinary contact with ordinary green things rather than a guided practice in a managed woodland. A recommendation a patient cannot follow is not care. It is a verdict, and it usually lands as one.

Convert the population claim into a personal experiment. This is the most useful thing in the chapter and it solves most of the dilemma. I cannot tell Ray what nature does for people in general with any precision. I can help him find out what it does for him. Pick one outcome you care about, and only one. Decide in advance what would count as a worthwhile change, in words, before you start: I want to fall asleep without lying there for an hour, or I want to get through Friday without the ache keeping me up. Pick a window, four weeks is usually right. Do the thing most days. At the end, decide. If nothing changed, stop without guilt, because you have learned something true about yourself rather than about a literature.

That move swaps a claim this research cannot support, this works, for one it does not need to support, let us see whether this works for you. And I should tell you its limits, because I have spent five chapters teaching you to ask about comparison groups and I am not going to hand you a design with no comparison group and pretend it is clean. A personal trial of one has no control. Ray's back may ease in those four weeks because the season changed, or because the flare was going to settle anyway, which is the drift toward the middle I warned you about in chapter 4, or because he expected it to. So the personal experiment is not proof. It is a decision tool, and the decision it informs is only whether Ray should keep doing a free and safe thing, which is a decision that does not require proof. Match the strength of your method to the stakes of the choice. For a cheap safe habit, this is enough. For stopping a medication, it is nowhere close.

Say what it does not replace, in the same breath. One sentence, every time. Keep taking the medication. We are still going to look at the knee. If the chest thing happens again you call, you do not walk it off. This costs you five seconds and it is the single sentence that separates a complementary recommendation from an accidental alternative one.

Let me put the two versions side by side so you can hear the difference, because this is what your sixth assignment is going to ask you to write.

Here is the dishonest version, dishonest in the way almost all of this writing is, which is that nothing in it is quite a lie. Research shows that spending time in nature lowers your stress hormones and boosts immune function. Two hours a week in a forest is clinically proven to improve sleep and mood. You should start forest bathing.

Count the failures. Tier four language on tier one evidence. A mechanism claim I cannot support presented as a finding. A dose nobody has established. Clinically proven, which means nothing. A practice Ray has no access to. No mention of what this does not replace. And an implied promise that will be falsified in three weeks, taking my credibility with it.

Here is the version I would actually say. Ray, here is what we know and what we do not. People who spend more time around green space tend to report sleeping better and feeling better, and that shows up over and over in the research. What nobody has really sorted out is whether it is the trees or just the walking and the daylight and being off your feet and away from a screen, because the people who get outside a lot are different from the people who do not in a hundred ways. So I am not going to promise you anything. What I will say is that it costs you nothing, it is safe for you, it will not interfere with anything else we are doing, and I notice that patients who get outside on a regular basis tend to tell me they are doing better. Try this. On your break, go stand or sit outside by the grass instead of staying in the break room. Do it most days for four weeks. Pick one thing to watch, and I would pick whether you are getting to sleep faster, since that is what is bothering you most. In a month you tell me whether anything changed. If nothing did, we drop it and no harm done. And none of this replaces what we are doing with the knee, so keep those appointments.

That is longer. It is also the version Ray will act on, because it tells him what to do tomorrow, in a place he can reach, with a way of finding out whether it worked. It contains no sentence I would have to walk back. It leaves my credibility available for the next thing I have to tell him, which may be urgent. And it took forty seconds.

Learn to write that paragraph. Not my words, yours, for your field and your patients. It is the most portable thing in this book, because the structure holds whatever the subject is: here is the pattern, here is what we cannot separate out, here is what it costs and risks, here is the smallest version that fits your life, here is how you will find out, here is what it does not replace.

Where the evidence runs out

Every chapter has this section. This one could run longer than the chapter, so I will keep to what matters most.

I cannot give you an effect size for nature exposure, and I have not, deliberately and by instruction. The literature our review draws on is largely observational, or small and short term, and the obvious confounders are rarely controlled. A number produced from that material would carry a precision the designs cannot support. Compare this with chapter 5, where I gave you 0.5 and 0.2 and insisted you memorize both, and notice that the difference is not my mood. It is the designs.

I cannot tell you whether nature is doing anything over and above the walking, the daylight, the quiet and the absence of a screen. That is the central question about the field, and answering it would require long randomized comparisons of equivalent activity in different settings. The short versions exist and are not enough. The long version is probably not possible.

I cannot tell you a dose. Not how long, not how often, not how green, not whether a city park counts the same as woodland, not whether looking out of a window does anything. Every confident dose you encounter in popular writing on this subject, and you will encounter several, is an extrapolation somebody made and then rounded into a prescription.

I cannot give you checked numbers for sleep, movement, diet or social connection, as I admitted when we got to them. Those literatures are large and I have read in them and they are not in our fact base, and a number I recalled rather than retrieved is the one unforgivable error in a book like this. Notice what this means: I have spent a third of a chapter teaching you to recommend four things I cannot quantify for you. I think the reasoning holds anyway. You are entitled to think the gap is a problem, and if you argue that in your assignment you will not lose a point for it provided you argue it well.

I cannot tell you whether my own practice is helping anyone. I tell patients to walk, to sleep, to eat better and to see their friends, I have done it for years, and I believe it matters. That belief is clinical observation by a man with an obvious stake in it, collected without a comparison group, remembered selectively, in a population that came to me because something hurt and left when it stopped. I would not accept that as evidence from anybody else, and you should not accept it from me. It is in this book as an example of the kind of thing a practitioner knows and cannot prove, which is a real category and deserves to be named rather than smuggled.

And I cannot tell you whether honest recommendations work as well as dishonest ones. I have argued that overselling costs you credibility and that credibility is a clinical instrument, and I believe that argument, and I notice it is exactly the sort of plausible mechanism story I spent the middle of this chapter telling you to be suspicious of. I have no trial comparing a careful explanation against a confident oversell for how many patients actually change what they do. If somebody runs it and the oversell wins, I will still teach the honest version, and I would be telling you a different kind of reason for it: not that it works better, but that the alternative is lying to a person who trusted you. That is not an evidence claim and I am not going to dress it as one.

Before you move on

  • Can you define confounding in plain language, without using the word variable, and give three specific confounders that would make a study of park use and health hard to interpret?
  • Can you explain the difference between a cross sectional study, a cohort study and a randomized trial, and say what each one can and cannot rule out?
  • Can you say why no study will ever randomize people to grow up with woodland behind the house, and connect that to why no trial has compared yoga with sham yoga?
  • Can you state the four tiers of claim from this chapter, from a pattern in the world to a dose and an effect size, and say which tier the nature literature currently supports?
  • Can you explain why forest bathing and green space are not the same object of study, and give an example of an argument that illegitimately moves from one to the other?
  • Can you write out, in your own words, a recommendation to a patient that is honest about what you do not know and that she would still act on, including the sentence saying what it does not replace?

Chapter 7

Telling Them Apart

Having read all of it, can you now build a comparison you would defend to someone who disagrees?

Every time I teach this course, somewhere around the start of the last week, I get a version of the same message. It arrives by email, usually late at night, usually from one of the students who has been working hardest. It says something like this: I have read all seven chapters and I still cannot tell you which category chiropractic goes in. Can you just give us the chart?

I want to answer that message properly, because the person sending it has not failed. She noticed something true, and she noticed it because she was paying attention. There is no chart. If there were a chart, I would have put it in chapter 1 and saved us all six weeks. What I have instead is something more useful and considerably more annoying: a method for building a chart you can defend, and a standard for judging whether somebody else's chart was built honestly.

That is what this chapter is for, and it is what your final assignment asks you to do. Choose three modalities. Choose your criteria. Place each modality against those criteria. Defend the criteria, not the placements. I grade the defense. I have given full marks to papers whose placements I disagree with, and poor marks to papers that landed exactly where I would have landed, because the reasoning underneath was a mess.

So the question at the center of the course catalog, how do you distinguish holistic wellness from western medicine and from alternative medicine, is about to get the only honest answer I have. You distinguish them by choosing criteria, applying them consistently, and accepting the placements that come out even when you do not like them. The interesting work is not in the distinguishing. It is in the choosing.

And let me say plainly what this chapter is not going to do, because the temptation in a final chapter is enormous and I can feel it. It is not going to resolve the course. A book that spent six weeks teaching you that the comparison decides the result, that mechanism and effect are separate variables, and that natural and safe are different words, cannot end by handing you a verdict without undoing its own work. What I can do is show you the through lines, state my own conclusions in my own name, and give you something to say on a Tuesday when a patient asks you a question you were not expecting.

Why the three categories are not natural kinds

Here is a phrase from philosophy that will do real work for you, so let me define it where it appears rather than send you to a glossary. A natural kind is a category that exists in the world rather than in our filing. Gold is a natural kind. Whether something is gold does not depend on who is looking, or what else is on the shelf, or what the buyer intends to do with it. You can be wrong about whether a lump is gold, and the lump does not care. Tests settle it. Holistic wellness, western medicine and alternative medicine are not categories of that sort, and I have been showing you why since the first chapter without using the phrase.

Start with the federal definitions, since they are the most carefully drawn ones in use and we have had them in hand since week one. The National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health defines complementary as a non mainstream approach used together with conventional medicine, alternative as a non mainstream approach used in place of conventional medicine, and integrative as a coordinated approach that brings conventional and complementary care together, often with several interventions at once, aiming to treat the whole person and to coordinate care among the providers involved.

Read those again with the natural kind test in mind. The first two do not describe treatments at all. They describe relationships. The same needle, in the same point, in the same shoulder, placed by the same acupuncturist on the same afternoon, is complementary in the patient who is also seeing her oncologist and alternative in the patient who stopped. Nothing about the needle has changed. What changed is a fact about the patient's other appointments, and the category tracks that fact rather than anything about the therapy.

That is already fatal to the idea that these are natural kinds, and it gets worse from there, in three ways the previous chapters established.

The first is that the boundary moves, for reasons that have nothing to do with the treatment. Remember the student from chapter 2, whose mother was treated for nausea by a licensed acupuncturist on the fourth floor of a hospital while my student saw a chiropractor in the strip mall four hundred feet away. Nobody ran a trial that put one inside the building and the other outside it. Remember Denise Alvarez, who crossed a state line for a job and found that her chiropractor's scope, her acupuncturist's supervision requirements and her sister's naturopath's legal existence had all changed, while her neck had not. What differed was which legislature wrote the rules. A category that reassigns a practice when a patient drives three hundred miles is not tracking a property of the practice.

The second is that the same practice sits in different categories depending on what it is being asked to do, and this is the point I most want you to take out of the course. Chiropractic is alternative medicine in a clinic where a parent is told that adjustments will manage her child's asthma, and it is routine musculoskeletal care in a clinic where a man with nine days of mechanical low back pain is examined, screened for the dangerous causes, told his spine is not broken, treated with hands and encouraged to keep moving. Same profession. Same license. Often the same technique. The two offices are not doing the same thing, because the claim being made is not the same claim, and the claim is what the evidence has to answer to. A field containing both of those offices cannot be given one placement on a chart.

The third is absorption, which chapter 2 treated as a historical process and which I want you to see as an ongoing one. Willow bark became aspirin. Hydropathy's sensible core, rest, graded movement, water, hygiene, attention to regimen, became physical therapy and rehabilitation and the whole modern apparatus of lifestyle advice. Osteopathy in the United States went further than any of them: the American osteopath became a physician who prescribes and operates and practices in every specialty, while in the United Kingdom and much of Europe and Australia the same word still names a regulated manual therapist who does neither. One word, two professions, depending on which country you are standing in. If a category can be crossed by a profession over eighty years, and can mean two different things in two countries at once, it is not carving nature at a joint.

Now the move I want to block, because it is the most common failure in the papers I read. Having discovered that the categories are constructed, the student concludes they are arbitrary, that any placement is as good as any other, and that the whole exercise is a word game. That does not follow. A county line is constructed and you can still be ticketed on the wrong side of it. A diagnosis of hypertension depends on a threshold a committee chose, the threshold has moved, people argue about where it should sit, and none of that makes blood pressure imaginary. Categories can be invented, revisable, contested and useful all at once. The test of a constructed category is not whether it exists in nature. It is whether the category does a job you can state, does it consistently, and whether you can say what would make you redraw it. That is the standard I will hold your criteria to.

Criteria people actually use, and which survive scrutiny

Here are the criteria I actually see, in papers, in clinic, in the popular books and in arguments at dinner. I will take each at its strongest, say what job it can do, and tell you whether I think it survives. The ones that fail do not fail because they are rude or unfashionable. They fail because they cannot be applied consistently without producing results nobody will accept.

One. Is it offered inside the institutions? Does a hospital provide it, does a license exist for it, does an insurer pay for it. This is the criterion most people use without noticing, and it has a real virtue: it is checkable, and the answers are public, which is more than you can say for most of what we have discussed.

It survives, but only if you are honest about what it measures. It measures institutional position, not efficacy, and chapter 2 was an argument about why. The decisive institutional victory in American medicine, the educational reform and the licensing acts and the consolidation of authority, happened substantially before the therapeutic revolution: the antitoxins, insulin, the sulfonamides and penicillin all arrived after the structure of professional dominance was already built. The winner's strongest evidence showed up after the match. And in 1987 a federal court found that the American Medical Association had engaged in an unlawful conspiracy to contain and eliminate the chiropractic profession, which establishes that organized exclusion is a documented event rather than a paranoid hypothesis. Label this criterion as a measure of position and you are on solid ground. Use it as a proxy for whether something works and you have confused what is permitted with what is true.

Two. Is the proposed mechanism compatible with established science? This survives, and it is stronger than students expect, provided you grade it rather than score it pass or fail. At least three different situations get lumped together under wrong mechanism, and they are not equivalent.

A mechanism can conflict with well established physical science. Homeopathy's claim that serial dilution increases potency is the clean example from chapter 2, and the strongest case in this book for a mechanism being not merely unproven but in conflict with what chemistry established about matter. A mechanism can be unsupported without conflicting with anything: the chiropractic subluxation is not absurd on its face, it is simply not demonstrated. And a mechanism can be a claim of a different kind altogether. Qi is not a substance practitioners claim you could isolate in a tube, and doshas are functional principles rather than a chemistry, which is why researchers proposing gate control or endorphin release for acupuncture are not testing the classical mechanism but offering a replacement that would preserve the practice. So the criterion survives in this form: is the mechanism unproven, incompatible, or stated in a vocabulary that does not meet the question. Three answers, three implications.

Three. What does the evidence show, and compared with what? This is the strongest criterion available to you, the one the whole course was built to teach, and I will spend a later section on it rather than this one. But take a warning about using it as a boundary criterion. Evidence quality does not sort cleanly by tradition. The cleanest result in this book, 39 trials and 20,827 patients analyzed from the original patient records, belongs to acupuncture, a component of a system whose physiology has not been demonstrated. Some of the murkiest evidence in the book attaches to spinal manipulation, the practice closest to mainstream musculoskeletal care and the one I perform. Evidence is a property of what has been studied and how, not of which door a practice came in through.

Four. Is it natural? Fails, and you should be able to dismantle it in one move. Natural is a claim about where something came from. Safe is a claim about what it does to a body at a given dose. The second does not follow from the first, and chapter 5 gave you the study that settles it: 70 ayurvedic herbal medicine products purchased from South Asian grocery stores in the Boston area, of which 14, one in five, contained lead, mercury and arsenic or some combination of them. Lead is natural. Lead is also a neurotoxin whose effects on a developing nervous system occur at low exposures and are not reversible. The criterion fails in the other direction too: digoxin came out of a plant and is now a tablet with a number on it, and if naturalness mattered, that tablet should worry you more than a preparation whose dose varies with the plant, the soil, the season and the batch. Drop this criterion. It flatters whichever side you already favored and does nothing else.

Five. Is it ancient? Fails, and chapters 4 and 5 both told you why. Age is not evidence of correctness: bloodletting had a long and distinguished run among intelligent people. Age is not evidence of incorrectness either: willow bark worked before anyone could say why. A claim that cannot settle anything in either direction is not a criterion. It is decoration.

Six. Is it popular? Fails as a criterion and survives as a fact you need. Use of these approaches among United States adults went from 19.2 percent in 2002 to 36.7 percent in 2022. None of that is evidence that any of it works. All of it is evidence that you will meet these patients, which is a different and equally important thing.

Seven. Does it treat the whole person? This fails as a boundary criterion, for the reason chapter 1 gave. Holistic is not the opposite of conventional. It describes a stance toward the patient, and the claim it makes, that body and mind and history and circumstances arrive as a package and that treating one while ignoring the others is bad practice, is a claim conventional medicine largely agrees with and frequently fails to act on. Ask any good family physician whether a patient's housing, sleep, work and relationships affect her health. You will get an immediate yes, followed by an account of a fifteen minute appointment slot. The complaint is about delivery, not doctrine. Try to separate the traditions with whole person care and you will find the line running through the middle of every one of them.

Eight. Is it being used with conventional care or instead of it? Survives, and it is the one I would put second after the evidence question. It is the federal distinction, it is about use rather than about the treatment, and chapter 1 argued that it is where the documented harms in this field cluster. Not because the treatments in the alternative column are more toxic, though a few are, but because the real injury is usually the conventional care that was declined. Dana's nine week old, brought for adjustments rather than weighed by a pediatrician. A practice with a 0.3 effect size substituted for treatment of a severe psychiatric illness. The same practice, with the same evidence behind it, is a reasonable addition in one patient and a dangerous substitution in another, and nothing about the practice changed between them.

Nine. Can the central finding be detected reliably? This criterion survives, it is badly underused, and it cuts across every tradition in the course including mine. If two trained practitioners examine the same patient, do they agree about what they find. Cranial osteopathy claims the bones of the skull retain a small rhythmic motion a trained hand can feel. Manual spinal assessment claims a practitioner can identify which segment needs treating. Ayurvedic pulse examination claims trained fingers can read qualities and interpret them as information about the doshas. Pattern differentiation claims a practitioner will arrive at the correct pattern. Every one of those is testable, and the logic is unforgiving: if the finding cannot be reliably detected, whatever is guiding the treatment comes from the practitioner rather than the patient, and the specificity story is finished regardless of whether the treatment helps. I have handed you no numbers, because this book's fact base holds no checked figures on diagnostic agreement, and this is probably the most important researchable question in the field.

Ten. Does the field have a brake on its own scope? Survives, and it is the criterion I weight most heavily after evidence and use, partly because it is the one I have watched fail from the inside. A field with a brake can say what its treatment does not do. A field without one drifts, and it drifts in the direction the mechanism predicts. If subluxation interferes with nerve transmission and nerves supply every organ, then adjusting a spine is a rational approach to asthma, ear infections, colic and high blood pressure. The logic is clean, it follows from the premise, and it is the profession's most serious failure. The practical form of this criterion is a question you can ask of any practitioner in any tradition, including me: what would you not treat, and what would make you change your mind.

Eleven. Does the practice come with dose control? Survives, and it is almost never on anybody's list. A tablet of a licensed drug has a number on it. A spoonful of a herbal preparation usually does not. My pharmacology training is what makes me insist on this one: every substance that does something useful at one dose does something harmful at another, and a practice that cannot tell you the dose cannot tell you where that line is. Notice that this criterion sorts within traditions rather than between them. It separates ayurvedic herbal products from ayurvedic dietary advice far more sharply than it separates ayurveda from anything else.

Twelve. Who bears the cost if you are wrong? This is not a classification criterion, and papers that use it as one get muddled. It is a decision criterion, and it belongs in the recommendation rather than in the chart. Chapter 5 put it plainly: when the evidence is thin and the harms are lopsided, you lean toward the side where being wrong is cheaper, and you say out loud that that is what you are doing. Being wrong in the reassuring direction about heavy metals falls on a child with a lead level. Being wrong in the cautious direction falls on an adult who buys a more expensive product. Those costs are not symmetrical. That is a clinical judgment rather than a scientific one.

Now the part that matters more than the list. Here are four tests any criterion has to pass before I will accept it in a paper, and they are the tests I actually apply when I grade.

First, can you state it without naming a tradition? A criterion that reads the mechanism must be compatible with physiology is a criterion. A criterion that reads it must not involve qi is a conclusion wearing a criterion's clothes.

Second, does it apply to conventional medicine, and will you accept the result? Run your criteria on a fifteen minute primary care appointment, on spinal fusion for non specific back pain, on long term opioid prescribing. If some conventional practices place badly, that is the criteria working. If they have been quietly built so that everything conventional passes and everything else does not, you have described your prior beliefs in a table.

Third, can it produce a placement you did not want? A criterion that has never once surprised you is not doing any work. In my own case, the evidence criterion places acupuncture for chronic pain better than it places the thing I do with my hands, because the acupuncture evidence exists in a form the manipulation evidence does not. I do not enjoy that. It is a sign the criterion is real.

Fourth, does the key word hold still? Chapter 3 gave this its proper name. Equivocation is when a word with two meanings appears twice in an argument, once in the sense that makes the premise true and once in the sense the conclusion needs, and because the spelling never changed, almost nobody notices the ground moved. Subluxation does it. Osteopath does it, in both directions. Works does it most of all, since it can mean patients feel better, or better than nothing, or better than the best available alternative. Write your criteria with definitions attached, then check that each word still means what it meant on page one.

Two students, two defensible maps

Abstractions are cheap, so let me do the assignment twice, with the same three modalities and different criteria, and show you that both versions can earn full marks. The three modalities are acupuncture for chronic pain, spinal manipulation for mechanical low back pain, and ayurvedic herbal products bought off a shelf. I chose those on purpose: they are where this book's evidence is most specific, and they include my own field so nobody can say I rigged the comparison.

The first student builds her chart on three criteria, in order: the quality of the comparison evidence, the support for the mechanism, and dose control.

On her chart, acupuncture for chronic pain comes out as the best supported practice of the three, and she says so even though she thinks the channel theory is mythology. Her defense is the evidence: 39 trials, 20,827 patients, individual patient records reanalyzed from scratch, acupuncture superior to both sham and no acupuncture for every pain condition studied with P less than .001, about 0.5 standard deviations against no acupuncture and about 0.2 against sham, still largely present at one year with about a 15 percent decline. She marks the mechanism separately and marks it weak, noting that the effect size varied mainly with what the control group received rather than with the technique used, which is the pattern you would expect if needle location were not the active component. Her chart reads: practice well supported, explanation poorly supported, and keeping those in separate columns is the whole point of her criteria.

Spinal manipulation for low back pain comes out in the middle of her chart, with an honest note that she cannot place it as precisely as she placed acupuncture. Her defense is that the evidence is genuinely harder to pin down, and she gives the reasons from chapter 3 rather than shrugging: manipulation is not one intervention, since trials labeled spinal manipulation have delivered thrusts, slow rhythmic mobilizations, instrument assisted taps and soft tissue work in various combinations by practitioners trained differently. There is no buildable sham for a thrust that takes a joint to the end of its range and produces an audible pop. And most episodes of ordinary low back pain improve substantially without treatment, which means regression to the mean, the tendency of anything measured at its extreme to be less extreme next time, is waiting to fool anybody who reasons from a clinic full of satisfied patients.

Ayurvedic herbal products come out worst on her chart, and here her third criterion does the work rather than her first. She is careful about what the heavy metal study supports: 70 products from South Asian grocery stores in one American metropolitan area, 14 containing lead, mercury or arsenic, nearly all manufactured in South Asia with 26 from India and one from Pakistan, and no United States manufactured comparison group, which means it cannot support a conclusion that imported products are worse than domestic ones. What it does support is that dose and content control are absent in a real sample of real products sold to real families. She places the products badly and places ayurvedic dietary and routine advice elsewhere entirely, because her criteria distinguish them.

The second student builds a different chart on different criteria: how the practice is used relative to conventional care, whether the field has a brake on its scope, and who bears the cost of an error.

On her chart, spinal manipulation for mechanical low back pain comes out as routine musculoskeletal care rather than alternative medicine, defended with the use criterion: the patient is declining nothing, and the practice sits among the reasonable first options for a condition whose realistic alternatives are medication with real risks, imaging that will probably find something incidental and frighten him, injections, surgery with its own uneven evidence, or nothing at all while he stays scared and stops moving. She then marks the same profession badly on her second criterion, because a field that has not retired a central claim it cannot support has no principled brake on scope. She places acupuncture as integrative rather than alternative, since in the setting she cares about it is delivered in a hospital, documented in a chart and coordinated with an oncologist, which is the federal definition almost word for word. And she places the herbal products as the genuinely alternative item, not because of the metals but because they are the part of the system most likely to be bought with no practitioner involved, so nobody is coordinating anything and the cost of an error falls on a patient whose clinician does not know what she is taking.

Two charts. Different placements for the same three practices. Both defensible, and both would earn full marks, because in each case the criteria were stated first, applied consistently, and allowed to produce a result the student did not pick in advance.

Now the version that fails. A paper places acupuncture as legitimate because a hospital offers it, chiropractic as alternative because insurers cover it inconsistently, and ayurvedic products as dangerous because they are unregulated. Three criteria, one per modality, each chosen after the placement was decided. That is not a comparison. It is three verdicts with reasons attached afterward, and the tell is that no criterion is ever applied to a modality it was not chosen for. A criterion you apply once is not a criterion.

Running the three questions across all six weeks

Now the through lines. You have been asking three questions since chapter 1: what is the claim, stated as a believer would state it; what is the proposed mechanism; and what does the evidence show, compared with what. Run each back across the whole book, because the patterns only become visible when you look at six weeks at once.

Question one, across the course. The first thing to notice is that the strength of the claim varies enormously, and it varies in a way that predicts how much trouble a tradition is in.

Massage makes the most modest claim in this book: that skilled work on soft tissue reduces pain and tension, improves ease of movement, lowers anxiety and improves a person's general sense of well being. Classical chiropractic makes a far larger claim, that interference with nerve transmission at a spinal segment impairs whatever those nerves supply, which licenses treatment of conditions anywhere in the body. Ayurveda and Traditional Chinese medicine make the largest claims of all, because they are complete systems rather than treatments. And the pattern is this: the more modest the claim, the less work the evidence has to do. Massage is in relatively little trouble because it promises relatively little. A system that promises to explain the whole body carries an enormous evidential burden, and carrying it is made harder because its own individualization is the claimed active ingredient, which is precisely what a conventional trial design cannot hold still.

The second thing to notice is that the claim a tradition makes about itself and the claim that gets tested are routinely different claims, which was chapter 4's central problem. Neither yoga nor meditation, in the traditions they come from, claims to be a treatment for a symptom. A serious meditation teacher will tell you the practice is often uncomfortable and that the point is a changed relationship to your own thoughts rather than a reduction in any particular one. What the clinical literature tests is a structured program of about eight weeks, measured with questionnaires the tradition did not choose, on a timescale a practitioner would call absurd. That mismatch is the price of admission to a clinic rather than a trick, and the traditions have mostly been willing to pay it. But it means a modest result on an anxiety score is a fact about eight weeks of taught meditation and an anxiety score. So the discipline question one leaves you with is this: whose claim are we assessing, theirs or the researchers'. Ask it every time.

Question two, across the course. Six weeks of mechanisms produce a ranking, and the ranking has an uncomfortable shape.

The best supported mechanisms in this book are the least exciting ones. For yoga, I put exercise first, and it is the mechanism least likely to appear in a studio's marketing: if you wanted to predict whether a yoga program would help a sedentary person's sore back, you would not need any yoga specific theory at all, only what we know about structured movement in deconditioned people. For taught programs of any kind, the non specific ingredients are enormous: expectation, a teacher's attention, a group, a ritual, a plausible explanation of your suffering, and the simple fact of doing something about a problem instead of enduring it. Non specific does not mean fake. A patient who feels better because somebody paid attention to her for an hour actually feels better.

The worst supported mechanisms, meanwhile, get the best press. Brain imaging is at the bottom of my list in chapter 4 and at the top in popular coverage: anything you do repeatedly for years is apt to show up in some measure of a brain, and a difference in an image does not tell you the difference caused the benefit, or that the benefit occurred, or what a patient should do on Monday. The toxin story offered for massage is not a mechanism at all, as three questions establish: which toxin, how is it measured, what was the level before and after. And the hypotheses offered for acupuncture, gate control, endorphin release and connective tissue signaling, are candidates of differing support rather than established accounts, which is why this book presents none of them as settled.

The second through line here is the most intellectually interesting thing in the course. A tradition can be right about what helps while being wrong about why. Those are separate claims, supported by separate evidence, deserving separate verdicts, and the most common error in writing about holistic health is treating them as one so that a verdict on either settles both. The enthusiast says patients improve, therefore the mechanism is correct. The skeptic says the mechanism is nonsense, therefore the treatment does nothing. Neither follows. Willow bark reduced fever for centuries before anybody had heard of a prostaglandin, and the bark did not start working when the chemistry was understood. It started being understood. But hold the sting that keeps that from becoming a free pass. Mechanism licenses extrapolation: knowing why something works tells you what else to try, and getting the why wrong makes your extrapolations fail in a predictable direction. You will confidently apply the treatment to conditions it cannot reach. That is the failure that put an infant on a table instead of a scale.

Question three, across the course. Here is where the book has a spine, and once you see the shape you will be able to predict it before you read a study.

Run the results together. Acupuncture for chronic pain: about 0.5 standard deviations better than no acupuncture, about 0.2 better than sham. Meditation programs, across 47 randomized trials and 3,515 participants restricted to trials with active controls: moderate evidence of improvement in anxiety at 0.38 falling to 0.22, depression at 0.30 falling to 0.23, and pain at 0.33, with no evidence that meditation programs were better than any active treatment such as drugs, exercise or other behavioral therapies. Yoga for chronic non specific low back pain: probably better than no exercise for pain and back related function at three months with small improvements, and probably little or no difference in back related function compared with other back focused exercise, with the evidence on pain for that comparison uncertain.

Three different traditions. Three different research groups. Three different methods. One shape. These practices do something, reliably, against doing nothing. None of them has been shown to beat the active alternative that was already available.

And last week extends the shape rather than breaking it. The nature based approaches arrive with a large pile of studies and a structural ceiling: a systematic review of the health and well being benefits of spending time in forests exists, and the literature behind that kind of question is largely observational or small and short term, with the obvious confounder, that people who walk in forests differ in many ways from people who do not, rarely controlled. Confounding is a third factor quietly influencing both the thing you changed and the thing you measured, and it is why chapter 6 was about how to recommend something honestly rather than about how much it works.

Now, three disciplines that question three imposes, each of which a careful student gets wrong at least once.

The first is that no evidence of superiority is not evidence of equivalence. Showing that two treatments are genuinely equivalent requires a trial built for that purpose, usually larger, with a stated margin for how much difference would count. A set of trials that failed to detect a difference may have been too small, or measured the wrong thing, or compared the two in patients where neither did much. Hold that distinction. People will try to take it from you, from both directions.

The second is that an effect size is a statement about a group rather than a promise to a person. An effect size of 0.3 means two group averages sit about a third of a typical spread apart, the piles of scores still overlap almost completely, and the average is compatible with everybody improving a little or with a quarter of people improving substantially and three quarters getting nothing.

The third is that a modest effect is not a negligible one. Modest but real is a position, it is probably the most common correct verdict in this field, and it is worth a great deal when the condition is common, the treatment is cheap and available, and the harms are small. The yoga harm data is the model: back pain was the most commonly reported harm, more frequent with yoga than with no yoga but similar to other exercise, with no suggestion of serious harm. That is exactly the harm profile that makes a modest benefit worth having.

And one last through line, which is the part of question three that no amount of research funding will fix. The top rung of the comparison ladder is unavailable for most of what we studied. There is no sham yoga, no sham meditation, and no buildable sham for a spinal thrust. Acupuncture is the exception in this book rather than the rule, which is exactly why it has the cleanest result and why the 0.5 and the 0.2 could be separated at all. The honest response to an unanswerable question is to stop making claims that require the answer.

What I think, and why you do not have to agree

I have been running a method for seven chapters and mostly keeping my conclusions out of it. Here they are, in first person, because you are entitled to know what your instructor believes and because a teacher who hides behind a method is a particular kind of coward. Read them as mine. Your paper is graded on your reasoning, and I mean that literally: a paper that argues against every one of the positions below, with criteria stated and applied consistently and evidence handled accurately, earns full marks. I have given full marks to such papers, and I enjoy them more than the ones that agree with me.

First, I think the three way distinction in the course title is nearly useless as a classification and genuinely useful as a diagnostic. Asking whether a practice is holistic, western or alternative tells me almost nothing. Asking what claim is being made, what explanation is offered, what comparison the evidence rests on, and whether this patient is adding something or declining something, tells me nearly everything I need.

Second, I think the most important variable in this field is not the tradition. It is whether the practice is being used alongside conventional care or instead of it. I said that in chapter 1 and six weeks of material have made me more confident rather than less. The documented harms cluster in the alternative column, and they cluster there mostly because of what was declined rather than because of what was taken. If I had to teach one sentence to every future clinician in the country, it would be that one.

Third, I think modest but real is the correct verdict for most of what we studied, and both the enthusiast and the skeptic find that unbearable for the same reason: it does not settle anything. Acupuncture for chronic pain, yoga for a sore back, meditation for anxiety, manual therapy for mechanical low back pain. In every case the honest reading is a real effect, modest in size, in the same neighborhood as the other real and modest things we have for those conditions. Which means the choice between them should turn on access, cost, risk and whether the patient will actually keep doing it, rather than on anybody's theory of healing. Adherence is not a footnote in chronic pain. It is most of the game.

Fourth, I think the mechanism stories in my own field are largely decorative. My expectation, labeled clearly as an expectation rather than a finding, is that if the sham study that cannot be built were built for spinal manipulation, the shape would look broadly like the acupuncture shape: a substantial benefit relative to doing nothing, and a much smaller portion of it attributable to the specific thing my hands do. That result would make most of the theory I was taught ornamental and most of the clinical value real, and I could live with it. Let me add something I have not said yet. My profession would be stronger, not weaker, for saying that out loud, because what we are currently protecting is not the patients and not the results. It is the story.

Fifth, I think the diagnostic reliability question is the most important open question in this field and that the traditions avoid it because they suspect the answer. That includes mine. If palpation cannot reliably locate the segment, if pulses cannot be reliably read, if patterns cannot be reliably differentiated, then the individualization these systems claim as their distinguishing virtue is not happening, whatever else is. I would rather know. I notice I am not certain I would rather know, which is worth telling you, because that is what motivated reasoning feels like from the inside when you catch it.

Sixth, I think a great deal of what makes these traditions work is the part nobody can patent: time, attention, touch, a coherent explanation, and permission to hope. That is not a deflationary conclusion. It is a criticism of a delivery system that has made those things scarce enough that people pay cash for them in a strip mall. If an hour of being taken seriously is therapeutic, and the evidence suggests something in that neighborhood is, the right response is not to sneer at the practitioners providing it. It is to ask why the fifteen minute appointment slot became the default, and who decided.

Seventh, the hardest one. I think the honest verdict on some of what we covered is that the evidence does not currently support the claims being made for it, and that saying so is part of respecting students who may enter these fields. Cranial technique has the furthest to go of anything in this book before its central finding has been shown to exist. The claims for spinal manipulation beyond the musculoskeletal system are not supported and the proposed mechanism does not survive examination. The visible minority within chiropractic that is hostile to vaccination is doing real harm, descends with depressing logic from the founding philosophy, and is in my judgment the single greatest discredit to a profession I otherwise defend. None of that is a verdict on a whole tradition, and I have tried hard not to let it become one. But a book that could not say any of it would have been a brochure.

Now the four places where a reasonable person most readily disagrees with me, stated the way a good opponent would state them, because you deserve the best version of the case against your instructor.

You might argue that I weight comparison evidence too heavily and clinical experience too lightly, and that a method which discounts what practitioners observe in favor of what fits a trial design has a built in bias toward whatever is easy to study. My answer is regression to the mean and the structure of the clinic, and I think my answer is right, but I notice it is also convenient for a man who likes reading studies. You might argue that I am too soft on chiropractic: compare the temperature of chapter 3 with chapter 5 and notice who got the benefit of the doubt. I asked you to make exactly that comparison on the first page and I meant it.

You might argue that I am too hard on whole systems, because I keep evaluating them with instruments they did not choose and then reporting that the instruments found little. Chapter 5 conceded part of this: if a system is doing something my outcome measure does not capture, my measure will not capture it, and the strongest version of your argument is that I substituted my outcomes for theirs and called the result a finding. And you might argue that institutional position deserves more weight than I give it, not as proof but as accumulated evidence of a kind, since a practice that survived decades inside a system with every incentive to discard what does not work has passed a test of a sort. I think chapter 2 answers that, because the timing runs the wrong way. But make the argument if you believe it.

What to say when a patient asks you

Now the practical chapter inside the chapter, because most of you will end up in a room with somebody who asks, and a framework is no use if it leaves you silent.

Start with the most important thing, which is a number from the first week. More than one adult in three used one of these approaches in 2022. In any clinical setting, a large fraction of the people in front of you are doing something in this book, and the only real question is whether they will tell you. They often will not, usually because they expect to be mocked. A clinician who signals contempt does not stop the patient using the thing. He stops the patient mentioning it, which is strictly worse, because some of these preparations interact with drugs and a few are genuinely dangerous in combination. Contempt does not stop the exposure. It hides it. So the first skill is not an answer. It is a face. Ask without one, every time.

Here is the set of questions I actually use, in roughly this order, and you can steal them.

What are you taking or doing, by name and by brand. This sounds pedantic and it is the most useful question on the list, because preparation and product are not interchangeable, a clinician who needs to look something up needs a name to look up, and the dose is not on the label in any usable form for most of these products.

What are you hoping it will do. This is the question that separates a complement from an alternative, and it does it without an interrogation. A patient who tells you she is doing it for the stress and the stiffness has told you she is adding. A patient who tells you she is doing it so she can come off her medication has told you something different, and she has told you kindly, in a way you can respond to.

What else are you doing, and what have you stopped. Not accusingly. People stop things for ordinary reasons, like the drive being shorter, which is what happened to the man in chapter 1 who drifted from a pain clinic to a chiropractor without anyone making a decision. Six months later his category had changed and nobody had chosen it.

Is it helping, and how would you know. This one starts the only experiment available in an individual patient. The averages are modest, the variation underneath them is probably large, and we cannot look at a patient and say in advance that she will be a responder.

Then the answers. Three situations cover most of what you will meet.

The patient who asks does it work. Do not answer as asked, because the word has at least three meanings and you will end up arguing about a different one than she meant. Ask what she wants it to do, then answer in the shape the evidence has. For yoga and a chronic sore back: this probably helps a bit more than doing no exercise, it has not been shown to help more than other exercise aimed at your back, and if you will actually go to the class, that is a good reason to pick it. For acupuncture and chronic pain: people who get acupuncture do meaningfully better than people who get nothing, somewhat better than people who get a convincing imitation of it, and the benefit was still mostly there a year later. For meditation and anxiety: reasonable evidence of modest help with anxiety, depression and pain, no evidence it beats medication or exercise or established talking therapies, and the improvement seems to fade when people stop.

Notice what those three answers have in common. Each one names the comparison. That is the whole trick, and it is why a clinician who has learned question three can answer honestly without either overselling or sneering.

The patient who is using something instead of care she needs. This is the one that matters most and the one where tone decides the outcome. Do not start with the evidence. Start with what she is trying to accomplish, because she almost always has a reason you would respect: a side effect she could not tolerate, a clinician who did not listen, a relative whose treatment went badly, or simple fear. Then be specific about what worries you, in terms of what might be lost rather than what she believes. And then, if you possibly can, keep the part that is not harming her. A patient who hears that the meditation is fine and worth continuing, and that the question is only whether it can carry the whole load for a severe illness, is far more likely to stay in the room than one who hears that all of it is nonsense. Where a product is involved, be concrete: one in five of a real sample of real products on real shelves contained lead, mercury or arsenic. That is not a reason to call her tradition poison. It is a reason to ask what she is taking, by name, every time.

The patient who asks what you think. This question deserves a straight answer, and a straight answer has three parts: what the evidence shows, with the comparison named; what you do not know, said plainly; and what you would do, labeled as your judgment rather than as a finding. That last distinction is the professional skill this book has been teaching under another name. I have modeled it on every page: here is what the study found, here is where the evidence stops, here is what I think, and the three are not the same kind of statement. Patients can tell when those are being blurred, and the blurring is what costs you trust later, when the thing you implied was established turns out not to be.

One final piece of advice, about the question you will dread. If a patient asks whether a treatment is safe and you do not have a number, say that you do not have a number. I have refused to supply several in this book, including for serious events after neck manipulation and for adverse events from acupuncture, because the estimates in circulation differ enormously depending on how cases were found and what denominator was used, and a safety figure is the last place I would guess. What I told you instead is what I do: ask about the specific warning features, use lower force approaches or decline to treat when something in a history is unusual, and treat a risk as real even when I cannot quantify it. If a practitioner tells you the risk is zero, he is not reading the literature. If a critic tells you it is common, neither is he.

Where the evidence runs out

Every chapter has had this section and this one has to cover the book.

The largest gap is the one I have circled back to all term. For most of what we studied, the specific and the non specific cannot be separated, and no design that exists will separate them. Acupuncture is the one place in this book where we can state the total effect and the specific effect as two numbers rather than one. For everything else we have the bundle, and anybody who tells you a practice has been shown to work through a specific mechanism, as opposed to working, is going beyond what the designs support.

The second gap is duration. The meditation numbers stop at three to six months. The yoga findings are at three months. The acupuncture result is the best of the lot, with a decline of about 15 percent at one year, and that is a single year for conditions people live with for decades. The research covers the first few weeks of a long relationship and then goes quiet.

The third gap is who. The averages are modest, the variation beneath them is probably large, and we cannot look at a patient and predict whether she is in the group this will help. That is the most clinically consequential hole in the field. It means advice at the population level and advice to the person in front of you are different tasks, and the second still comes down to a careful try and an honest look.

The fourth gap is diagnostic reliability, named for the third time because it deserves it. There is no checked figure in this book's fact base on how well practitioners in these traditions agree with each other. It is researchable. Somebody should research it.

The fifth gap bears on this chapter in particular, and it is the most honest thing I can say about my own framework. I cannot tell you that my criteria are the right criteria. I gave you twelve that people use and argued that most survive scrutiny, and that argument rests on four tests I chose: statability without naming a tradition, applicability to conventional medicine, the ability to surprise its author, and stability of the key terms. Those tests are not findings and they are not in the fact base, because they could not be. They are a working philosophy of how to argue carefully, assembled by a clinician who has watched a lot of bad arguments in this field. If your paper takes one of the four apart, that is a legitimate paper and I will read it with real interest.

The sixth is historical and I flagged it in week two. The weighting of the four forces is unresolved, the counterfactual is unknowable because no state ran the alternative for eighty years, and Starr's central claim about cultural authority is an argument I handed you secondhand and have not verified against his text, which is why there is not a single quotation from him in this book. If you build on it, go read it, and tell me if I have it wrong.

And the seventh is about me, so it belongs last. I have told you where I stand five or six times, asked you to read chapter 3 harder than the others, and tried throughout to hold my own field to the standard I applied to everything else. I cannot certify that I succeeded. Motivated reasoning does not announce itself, it works on careful and decent people, and the only known defense is for somebody else to check the work. That is not a flourish at the end of a textbook. It is the reason your final assignment is built the way it is. You are the check.

Before you move on

  • Can you explain what a natural kind is, and give three specific reasons from this book that holistic wellness, western medicine and alternative medicine are not natural kinds, without concluding that the categories are therefore arbitrary?
  • Can you name at least four criteria people use to sort these practices, say which of them survive scrutiny and which do not, and give the reason each failing criterion fails?
  • Can you state the four tests a criterion has to pass, and apply them to one criterion you intend to use in your own paper?
  • Can you run the three questions across the whole course and state the recurring shape of the evidence in one sentence, with the acupuncture, meditation and yoga results as support?
  • Can you summarize your instructor's own conclusions, give the strongest argument against any two of them, and say what evidence would decide between you?
  • If a patient told you tomorrow that she had stopped a prescribed medication and was managing with a traditional preparation her aunt sends her, what would you ask her first, what would you check, and what would you say?
